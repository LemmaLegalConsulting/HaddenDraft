II. LAW AND ARGUMENT

A. THE SUMMARY JUDGMENT STANDARD

Plaintiff has failed to carry her burden of setting forth specific facts and demonstrating genuine issues of material fact regarding each allegation of her complaint. See, Civ.R. 56(E).

A complete failure of proof concerning an essential element of the nonmoving party's case necessarily renders all other facts immaterial. As a result, a moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law where the nonmoving party failed to come forth with evidence of specific facts on an essential element of the case with respect to which he has the burden of proof. Black Cosentino (1996), 117 Ohio App.3d 40, 43 (citing Celotex Corp. v. Catrett (1986), 477 U.S. 317, 323).

In responding to a motion for summary judgment, the nonmoving party may not rest on bare allegations to demonstrate a factual dispute. Dresher v. Burt (1996), 75 Ohio St.3d 280, 293, 1996 Ohio 107, 662 N.E.2d 264. Additionally, a summary judgment motion must be overcome by specific and provable facts and not mere allegations; evidence of a possible inference is insufficient. Jackson v. Alert Fire and Safety Equip., Inc. (1991), 58 Ohio St.3d 48, 52; Cox v. Commercial Parts Serv. (1994), 96 Ohio App.3d 417, 421. The key to the granting of summary judgment motion is that there are no genuine issues as to a material fact. Material facts are determined by substantive law. Only disputes over facts that might affect the outcome of a suit under governing law will properly preclude the grant of a motion for summary judgment. Irrelevant and unnecessary factual disputes will not be counted. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 247-248; Perez v.

Scripps-Howard Broadcasting (1988), 35 Ohio St.3d 215.

Here, plaintiff cannot set forth any evidence showing a material issue of fact for a jury to determine. Thus, defendants are entitled to summary judgment on each of plaintiff's claims. B. NEGLIGENCE

To prevail in a negligence action, the plaintiff must prove the 1) the existence of a duty, 2) a breach of that duty, and 3) an injury proximately resulting from the breach of that duty. Menifee v. Ohio Welding Prods., Inc. (1984), 15 Ohio St.3d 75, 77, 472 N.E.2d 707. Plaintiff has failed to produce evidence that satisfies each of these three prongs.

1. Plaintiff was not a tenant at the time of the incident.

Plaintiff was not a party to the lease between Defendants and Mr. Morrison. (Exhibit A) . Defendants Mark and Charlene Houser initiated eviction proceedings against their tenant Patrick Morrison and Plaintiff on December 11, 2005. Mr. Houser posted the required three-day notice to leave the premises at the property, naming both Mr. Morrison and Plaintiff, as well as "all other occupants." (Exhibit C) Plaintiff's disingenuous affidavit to the contrary is insufficient to create a genuine question of material fact. By her own admission, Plaintiff did not pay any rent to the Housers. (Plaintiff's Depo. at page 13). Furthermore, the fact that Mr. Houser may at one time have considered Plaintiff to be a tenant has no impact on the legal relationship between the two parties at the time of the incident. Defendants owed no duty to the Plaintiff other than to refrain from willful, wanton or reckless conduct. Under Ohio law, a landlord owes the same duty to a guest of a tenant, lawfully on the premises, as he does to the tenant. (emphasis added) Shump v. First Continental-Robinwood Assocs. (1994), 71 Ohio St. 3d 414, 419, 644 N.E.2d 291, 294. As noted above, the Housers had initiated eviction proceedings against the tenant, Mr. Morrison, and all the other residents of 273 Cranz, including Plaintiff. Because plaintiff was not lawfully on the premises at the time of the incident, Shump is inapplicable.

Therefore, the Housers owed Plaintiff only the duty they owed to any other licensee "who enters the premises of another by permission or acquiescence, for his own pleasure or benefit." Light v. Ohio Univ. (1986), 28 Ohio St.3d 66, 68, 28 Ohio B. 165, 502 N.E.2d 611. That duty is merely to refrain from willful, wanton or reckless conduct that is likely to injure him. See Gladon v. Greater Cleveland Regional Transit Auth. (1996), 75 Ohio St.3d 312, 315, 1996 Ohio 137, 662 N.E.2d 287.

2. The Housers are not liable for Plaintiff's injuries, pursuant to R.C. 5321.04

The Ohio legislature created the Landlord-Tenant act to regulate residential property rental relationships. R.C. §) 5321.04(A)(2) provides that a "landlord who is a party to a rental agreement shall (1) Comply with the requirements of all applicable building, housing, health, and safety codes that materially affect health and safety; (2) Make all repairs and do whatever is reasonably necessary to put and keep the premises in a fit and habitable condition."

A violation of a building code does not constitute negligence per se and is not conclusive proof of a party's negligence. Chambers v. St. Mary's School (1998), 82 Ohio St.3€6 563, 568, 1998 Ohio 184, 697 N.E.2d 198. Rather, evidence of a code violation may be 'taken into consideration along with all other materials correctly submitted pursuant to Civ.R. 56(E) in order to determine whether a party was negligent. Id. An alleged violation of R.C. 5321.04 does not obviate a plaintiff's obligation to prove that the violation was the proximate cause of the injury complained of and that the landlord had notice of the defective condition. Shroades v. Rental Homes, Inc. (1981), 68 Ohio St. 24 20, 25-26, 427 N.E.2d 774."

Ohio law recognizes contributory negligence as "any want of ordinary care on the part of the person injured, which combined and concurred with the defendant's negligence and contributed to the injury as a proximate cause thereof, and as an element without which the injury would not have occurred." Stiver v. Miami Valley Cable Council (1995), 663 N.E.2d 1310, quoting Brinkmoeller Wilson (1975), 41 Ohio St.2d 223, 226, 325 N.E.2d 233.

Plaintiff walked, in her bare feet, through the snow to get the blinds before she returned to the house with wet feet. (Plaintiff's Depo. at page 38). She was able to safely traverse the steps on the way down. The only difference between then and when she walked back up them was that her bare feet were wet from the snow and her hands were full. Her own negligence is solely responsible for her injuries.

5. Defendants Mark and Charlene Houser were not in exclusive possession and control over the area where the Plaintiff fell.

The evidence in this case is undisputed that the tenant, Patrick Morrison and the Plaintiff had exclusive possession and control of the leased property, and, further, were responsible for rehabilitating and maintaining the steps where plaintiff fell. Mark Houser stated in his deposition

that "Mr. Morrison, being a rehab guy, was going to perform some of the needed work on the house." (See Defendant Mark Houser's Deposition which has been filed with the court at page 9). In fact, Mr. Houser specifically directed Mr. Morrison to fix the things that were on the list of repairs required by the Health Department. (Houser Dep. at page 10). "It has been the rule in Ohio that a landlord out-of-possession is not liable to persons injured by reasons of the acts or failures of the tenant." Forester v. FL. M., Inc. (1978), 60 Ohio App.2d 342. The lessor who does not retain the right to admit or exclude others from the premises has generally not reserved the degree of possession or control necessary to impose liability for the condition of the premises. Pifts v. Housing Authority (1953), 160 Ohio St. 129, 113 N.E.2d 869 ; Cooper v. Roose (1949), 151 Ohio St. 316, 85 N.E.2d 545. The testimony of defendant Mark Houser clearly establishes that Morrison had taken the responsibility to maintain the property and do necessary repairs. Accordingly, summary judgment is also appropriate on this ground.

However, even if the court finds that the Housers were in possession or cortrol of the property, they are not liable for Plaintiff's injuries.

In Shump, the plaintiff decedent had been a social guest of the tenant, who succumbed to smoke inhalation allegedly due to the landlord's failure to properly install a smoke detector in the apartment, in violation of a city ordinance. 71 Ohio St. 3d at 415. The Ohio Supreme Court held in that case"the common-law classifications of trespasser, licensee, and invitee determine the legal duty that a tenant owes others who enter upon rental property that is in the exclusive control of the tenant. However, with regard to areas within the exclusive possession of a tenant, the common-law classifications do not affect the legal duty that a landlord owes a tenant or others lawfully upon the leased premises." [emphasis added), /d. at 417. Unless the court finds that Plaintiff was in control of the area where she fell, Shump is inapplicable and the Housers owe only a common law duty to

refrain from wanton or willful conduct.

Additionally, the Ninth Appellate District has declined to extend the Shump holding to cases involving injuries suffered from a mishap occurring in a common area. See, e.g. Sanders v. Bellevue Manor Apts.(Jan. 3, 1996), Lorain App. No. 95CA¥006A067; Rios v. Shauck (June 3, 1998), Lorain App. No. 97CA006753. In those cases, the court held that as to common areas, the coramon law duty of care toward a licensee still applies, namely that the landlord will refrain from wanton and willful misconduct. While the property in question is a single-family unit, the outside entrance, walkway, and steps would generally be considered a common area in a multi-unit facility. As a matter of policy, there is no incentive to treat the two differently and this Court should accordingly grant summary judgment in favor of the Housers.
