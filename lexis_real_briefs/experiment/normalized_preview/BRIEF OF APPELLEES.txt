STATEMENT OF FACTS

This case rests on the fact that, on September 21, 1999, Appellee John Pavlik was accorded a final judgment for $ 24,625.35 against Appellant Peter Pavlik in a Complaint in Forcible Entry and Detainer, Possession of Property, Past Due Rent and Property Damage in the Court of Common Pleas of Cuyahoga County, Case No. CV-99-378656 involving the property at 4864 Chaincraft Road, Garfield Heights, Ohio. This is the exact same real property which is at issue here. Appellant Peter Pavlik appealed this decision and the decision was affirmed. The complaint for Quiet Title and the Affidavit of Facts filed herein by Appellant Peter Pavlik is simply another attempt by Appellant Peter Pavlik to change the decision already rendered in the prior case. It is interesting to note that in his Appellant's Brief, in the Statement of the Case and Statement of the Facts which encompass over seven pages, Appellant Peter Pavlik never even mentions this prior case.

This litigation, in one case or the other, has been going on for over eight years and, as a result, the facts are somewhat convoluted. John and Peter Pavlik, Appellee and Appellant herein, are brothers and were business partners. Appellant Peter Pavlik, d.b.a. GPV performance, Inc., repaired and serviced automobiles at 4876 Chaincraft Road and Appellee John Pavlik ran the administrative aspect of the business. Joseph Cassalicchio also worked with Peter Pavlik repairing automobiles. The property at 4876 Chaincraft Road is next door to the property at 4864 Chaincraft Road which is the subject of the Quiet Title Action herein. Appellee John Pavlik has been the owner of the property at 4864 Chaincraft Road since October 21, 1994, over twelve years.

The business relationship between John and Peter Pavlik existed until 1998, when the relationship between John and Peter Pavlik deteriorated due to the drug use on the part of Peter Pavlik. At this time, Joseph Casalicchio, as a result of a rental agreement, and Peter Pavlik, were storing cars in Appellee John Pavlik's building at 4864 Chaincraft Road. Appellee John Pavlik requested that Joseph Casalicchio remove the cars and repair parts being stored there and he refused. Appellee John Pavlik filed a complaint on October 26, 1998, in the Garfield Heights Municipal Court to evict Joseph Casalicchio from the property and recover damages. Joseph Casalicchio filed a counterclaim in excess of $ 30,000.00 and he requested that the case be certified to the Court of Common Pleas. The case was transferred as requested, Case No. CV-99-378656. Appellee John Pavlik then filed an amended complaint in the Court of Common Pleas naming Appellant Peter Pavlik and GPV Performance, Inc. as new party defendants because Peter Pavlik was also storing items at 4864 Chaincraft Road. Joseph Casalicchio and Peter Pavlik moved the items out voluntarily, but they damaged the building when they moved out. This is the same building Appellant Peter Pavlik now alleges he owns, and these damages resulted in a $ 24,625.35 judgment against Peter Pavlik and GPV Performance, Inc.

Appellant Peter Pavlik, although service was perfected upon him, failed to answer and/or defend the Complaint, Case No. CV-99-378656. Joseph Casalicchio did defend, and he and his attorney agreed to binding arbitration. A binding arbitration hearing was held on February 7, 2000 and Appellant Peter Pavlik appeared at the hearing to testify on behalf of Joseph Casalicchio. It is interesting to note that Appellant Peter Pavlik never even alleged that he had any ownership interest in the property. The arbitrators issued a finding for Appellee John Pavlik and ordered Joseph Casalicchio to pay $ 4,800.00. Joseph Casalicchio filed exceptions to the Report and Award of Arbitrators and these exceptions were denied.

On February 28, 2000, Appellee John Pavlik filed a judgment lien on the building owned by Appellant Peter Pavlik at 4876 Chaincraft Road for the $ 24,625.35 judgment amount. On March 28, 2000, Peter Pavlik and GPV Performance, Inc. filed a Motion for Relief from Judgment which was denied. Appellant Peter Pavlik appealed the denial and the judgment was affirmed. It is interesting to note that oral argument was had on his appeal on November 5, 2001 and Appellant Peter Pavlik and his attorney failed to appear.

While the appeal was pending, on March 2, 2000, Appellant Peter Pavlik filed an Affidavit of Facts Relating to Title (No. 200003020388) on the property at 4864 Chaincraft Road alleging that either he owned the property or a corporation called Philip Panic, Inc. owned the property. Appellant Peter Pavlik alleged that he was the sole shareholder of Philip Panic, Inc.

During the course of their business relationship, Appellant Peter Pavlik and Appellee John Pavlik formed several corporations for business purposes. Among these corporations were Philip Panic, Inc. and Penny Savers, Inc. Philip Panic, Inc. was incorporated in 1989 in Delaware and the charter became inactive on September 10, 1993, and was not revived until March 21, 2001, for the Quiet Title Action. In fact, the charter was inactive on October 21, 1994, when Appellee John Pavlik purchased the building at 4864 Chaincraft Road and the deed to the property was recorded.

On March 27, 2001, Appellants Peter Pavlik and Philip Panic, Inc. filed a Complaint to Quiet Title against Appellee John Pavlik as Case No. CV-01-433601. Appellee John Pavlik filed his answer which included affirmative defenses and a counterclaim. This counterclaim involved the property at 4876 Chaincraft Road.

On November 30, 2001, Appellee John Pavlik filed a Motion for Summary Judgment on the basis of res judicata and collateral estoppel. Appellant Peter Pavlik responded on January 23, 2002. On January 29, 2002, additional exhibits, including the Court of Appeals decision, were filed in reference to the Motion for Summary Judgment. Despite repeated requests, a decision was not rendered on the Motion for Summary Judgment and the case stalled. The case was then stayed because Appellant Peter Pavlik filed a Chapter 13 Bankruptcy and this resulted in an automatic stay of the court proceedings. When the Appellant Peter Pavlik's Chapter 13 Bankruptcy was dismissed, Appellee John Pavlik filed a Supplemental Motion for Summary Judgment on March 23, 2004, essentially the same as the initial Motion for Summary Judgment, in another attempt to move the case forward.

In June of 2004, Appellant John Pavlik was informed that Penny Savers, Inc. had filed a Complaint in Forcible Entry and Detainer Against Appellant Peter Pavlik in regard to the adjacent real estate located at 4876 Chaincraft Road, Garfield Heights, Ohio, as Case No. CV-04-529853. Since this property was also the subject of the counterclaim in Case No. CV-01-433601, Appellee John Pavlik filed a motion to consolidate the two cases. The Court granted this motion over the objection of Appellant Peter Pavlik on July 2, 2004.

In March 2005, with still no decision on the Motions for Summary Judgment, the consolidated cases were once again stayed because Appellant Peter Pavlik filed yet another Chapter 13 Bankruptcy. This Chapter 13 Bankruptcy was dismissed, again, and Appellee John Pavlik filed a motion to reinstate the case on August 15, 2006 and this motion was granted on August 22, 2006.

A pre-trial was had and additional supplemental responses were filed in regard to the Motion for Summary Judgment originally filed on November 30, 2001. Finally, on November 28, 2006, Appellee John Pavlik's Motion for Summary Judgment was granted. Plaintiff/Appellant Peter Pavlik appealed herein.

Unfortunately, Appellee John Pavlik has been unable to sell his property at 4864 Chaincraft Road because he is unable to grant good title due to the Affidavit Against the Title and this pending action.

LAW AND ARGUMENT

A. Summary Judgment

The standard for summary judgment is as follows:

Before summary judgment may be granted, it must be determined that (1) no genuine issue as to any material fact remains to be litigated, (2) the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, and (3) it appears from the evidence that reasonable minds can come to but one conclusion, and viewing such evidence most strongly in favor of the nonmoving party, that conclusion is adverse to the party against whom the motion for summary judgment is made. Temple v. Wean United, Inc. (1977), 50 Ohio St.2d 317, 327.

Summary judgment is appropriate in this case on a res judicata and collateral estoppel basis since a money judgment for $ 24,625.35 was granted in the Court of Common Pleas of Cuyahoga County Case No. 378656 against Appellant Peter Pavlik for damages to the property at 4864 Chaincraft Road, a property which he is herein alleging he owns. It is important to note that all issues relating to the property at 4864 Chaincraft Road are resolved via the granting of this Motion for Summary Judgment. The only remaining issues in the case relate to the property at 4876 Chaincraft Road. The rights of both Appellants Peter Pavlik and Philip Panic, Inc were addressed in the Motion for Summary Judgment and the Affidavit of Facts Relating to the Title of the property at 4864 Chaincraft Road must be withdrawn.

B. Res Judicata and Collateral Estoppel

Plaintiffs/Appellants Peter Pavlik and Philip Panic, Inc. are alleging an ownership right in 4864 Chaincraft Road, Garfield Heights, Ohio. This is the same property that was at issue in Cuyahoga County Court of Common Pleas Case No. 99-378656.

The Court in Grava v. Parkman Twp. (1995), 73 Ohio St.3d 379 held that "a valid, final judgment rendered upon the merits bars all subsequent actions based upon any claim arising out of the transaction or occurrence that was the subject matter of the previous action." (Paragraph two of the syllabus of Norwood v. McDonald (1943), 142 Ohio St. 299, 27 O.O. 240, 52 N.E.2d., overruled; Paragraph two of the syllabus of Whitehead v. Gen. Tel. Co. (1969). 20 Ohio St.2d 108, 49 O.O. 2d 435, 254 N.e.2d 10, overruled to the extent inconsistent herewith, paragraph one of the syllabus of Norwood, supra, and paragraph one of the syllabus of Whitehead, supra, modified. 1 Restatement of the Law 2d, Judgments (1982), Sections 24-25, approved and adopted.)

Plaintiffs/Appellants, in their brief to this Court, cited Norwood v. McDonald (1943), 142 Ohio St. 299. However, as stated above, the Ohio Supreme Court in Grava v. Parkman Twp. (1995), 73 Ohio St.3d 379 specifically overruled paragraph two of the syllabus of Norwood which states "A judgment or decree in a former action does not bar a subsequent action where the causes of action are not the same, even though each action relates to the same subject matter." Res judicata applies to that which was determined and to those questions that might properly have been litigated. Stromberg v. Bratenahl Bd. of Edn. (1980), 64 Ohio St.2d 98. In Grava, (supra), the Ohio Supreme Court held that res judicata "applies to extinguish a claim by plaintiff against the defendant even though the plaintiff is prepared in the second action (1) To present evidence or grounds or theories of the case not presented in the first action, or (2) To seek remedies or forms of relief not demanded in the first action." Grava, 73 Ohio St.3d at 383, quoting 1 Restatement of the Law 2d, Judgments (1982) 209, Section 25. Any issues concerning ownership of the property at 4864 Chaincraft Road should have been raised by Appellant Peter Pavlik in Case No. CV-99-378656.

Plaintiffs/Appellants, on page 14 of Appellants' Brief, state that Appellee "John Pavlik never even asked the lower court to find that he was the owner of the real estate, nor did he ever claim to be such in his pleadings". Appellee John Pavlik asked the court to order Appellants to remove their possessions from his property and grant him compensatory damages for the destruction to his property caused by Appellant Peter Pavlik. Appellee John Pavlik knew that he owned the property at 4864 Chaincraft Road and he knew that the deed showed that he was the owner. There was never a question concerning the ownership of the property because all parties involved understood that Appellee John Pavlik owned the property. The more important question is why Appellant Peter Pavlik never raised the issue of ownership and why he would damage his own property.

Plaintiffs/Appellants cite several cases which distinguish forcible entry and detainer actions from other causes of action. These cases, however, are extremely old and most deal with the jurisdiction of a justice of the peace or Municipal Court to hear cases involving forcible entry and detention separate from a complaint for monetary damages. In Appellant's Brief, Appellant cited Carroll v. O'Connor (1874), 25 Ohio St. 617, for the premise that an action in Forcible Entry and Detainer is solely a possessory action. In Carroll, the Ohio Supreme Court held that "Application is not authorized to be made to this court to review directly the proceedings of a justice of the peace in forcible entry and detention, or of forcible detention only, notwithstanding leave to file a petition in error in the Common Pleas may have been refused." While this is interesting from a historical perspective, it is not applicable to the case at bar because Case No. CV-99-378656 also involved a complaint for compensatory damages. There was no eviction order in this case because the Defendants had voluntarily vacated the premises. Unfortunately, they damaged the building in the process and this resulted in the monetary judgment.

In Behrle v. Beam (1983), 6 Ohio St.3d 41, also cited by Appellants, the Court held that "In a cause seeking forcible entry and detainer, an action at law upon a contract, when a counterclaim raises equitable issues involving the right of possession under an exercise of an option of purchase within the contract, a minicipal court may, pursuant to R.C. 1901.18, retain jurisdiction of the matter and hear and determine all legal and equitable issues and remedies for the complete determination of the rights of the parties."

The Court in Ft. Frye Teachers Assn., OEA/NEA v. State Emp. Relations Bd. (1998), 81 Ohio St.3d 392 stated, "It has long been held that the legal doctrine of res judicata consists of two related concepts-claim preclusion and issue preclusion, quoting Grava v. Parkman Twp. (1995), 73 Ohio St.3d 379. The Court in Ft. Frye Teachers Assn., OEA/NEA further stated "the doctrine of issue preclusion, also known as collateral estopel, holds that a fact or a point that was actually and directly at issue in a previous action, and was passed upon and determined by a court of competent jurisdiction, may not be drawn into question in a subsequent action between the same parties or their privies, whether the cause of action in the two actions be identical or different.***While the merger and bar aspects of res judicata have the effect of precluding relitigation of the same cause of action, the collateral estoppel aspect precludes the relitigation, in a second action of an issue that has been actually and necessarily litigated and determined in a prior action that was based on a different cause of action. In short, under the rule of collateral estoppel, even where the cause of action is different in a subsequent suit, a judgment in a prior suit may nevertheless affect the outcome of the second suit."

Appellants Peter Pavlik and Philip Panic, Inc. are in privity with each other when considering the application of res judicata and collateral estoppel. "In certain situations *** a broader definition of privity is warranted. As a general matter, privity is merely a word used to say that the relationship between the one who is a party on the record and another is close enough to include the other within the res judicata." Brown v. Dayton (2000), 89 Ohio St.3d 245 quoting Bruszewski v. United States (C.A.3, 1950), 181 F.2d 419, 423; Thompson v. Wing (1994), 70 Ohio St.3d 176, 184.

In Brown (supra), the Court found "that a mutuality of interest, including an identity of desired result, creates privity between the plaintiffs..." The Court in Brown further stated, "Whether the original claim explored all the possible theories of relief is not relevant. It has long been the law of Ohio that an existing final judgment or decree between the parties to litigation is conclusive as to all claims which were or might have been litigated in a first lawsuit.***The doctrine of res judicata requires a party to present every ground for relief in the first action, or be forever barred from asserting it. " Natl. Amusements, Inc. v. Springdale (1990), 53 Ohio St.3d 60, 62, quoting Rogers v. Whitehall (1986), 25 Ohio St.3d 67.

Appellant Philip Panic, Inc. and Appellant Peter Pavlik are clearly in privity with one another and the doctrine of res judicata and collateral estoppel applies to both of them.

CONCLUSION

As was stated exhaustively, if Appellants wished to raise factual issues in regard to the ownership of the property at 4864 Chaincraft Road, they should have done so in the prior litigation. The prior litigation clearly dealt with the issue of ownership of the property because a person would not be ordered to pay damages for harm they did to their own property. This action is nothing more than an attempt to have another opportunity to litigate the issues which should have been raised in the prior action.
