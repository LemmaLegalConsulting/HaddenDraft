STATEMENT OF THE CASE

On December 16, 2005, defendant-appellant pleaded guilty to pandering sexually oriented matter involving a minor and possession of criminal tools. The docket in Cuyahoga County Common Pleas Court, Case No. CR-05-471208, was attached as Exhibit "A" to defendant's response to plaintiff's motion for summary judgment and defendant's motion for summary judgment filed on September 26, 2006.

On April 12, 2006, plaintiff-appellee, the City of Lyndhurst, Ohio, filed a complaint and request for temporary restraining order and injunction alleging that defendant-appellant resided within 1,000 feet of a school and that pursuant O.R.C. 2950.031, defendant-appellant was not authorized to occupy the residential premises and would need to vacate the premises. The Code section provides that if an individual has pleaded guilty to a sexually oriented offense that is not a registration-exempt sexually oriented offense, that person shall not occupy a residential premises - within 1,000 feet of any school premises. The statute further provides that if the section is being violated, the City has a cause of action for injunctive relief against the person. The City, along with the complaint, filed a separate motion for a temporary restraining order and motion for a permanent injunction. On April 25, 2006, defendant-appellant timely filed an answer and specifically asserted that he did not occupy a residential premises within 1,000 feet of any school premises, as of January 31, 2006, the date he was sentenced pursuant to the plea entered on December 16, 2005. On or about August 11, 2006, the City of Lyndhurst, Ohio, filed a motion for summary judgment. On September 26, 2006, defendant-appellant filed a response to plaintiff's motion for summary judgment and his own motion for summary judgment. On December 13, 2006, without a hearing, the trial court issued a journal entry and opinion, including findings of fact and conclusions of law. The trial court found that defendant was classified as a sexual predator, that the South Euclid-Lyndhurst Board of Education owned the property known as Sunview School; that the School Board owned a one-half interest in a 10-foot wide pedestrian walkway; that the pedestrian walkway was traversed by children going to and from school and that defendant resided within 1,000 feet from the school. Therefore, the court held that defendant-appellant was enjoined from residing at 5496 Lansbury Lane, in Lyndhurst, Ohio, pursuant to R.C. 2950.031 (A). On January 9, 2007, defendant-appellant filed a timely notice of appeal.

ARGUMENT

I. THE TRIAL COURT IMPROPERLY GRANTED APPELLEE'S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE THERE IS A GENUINE ISSUE OF MATERIAL FACT REMAINING AS TO WHETHER OR NOT DEFENDANT-APPELLANT RESIDED WITHIN 1,000 FEET OF A SCHOOL AS OF JANUARY 31, 2006, THE DATE HE WAS SENTENCED ON HIS PLEA OF GUILTY TO PANDERING SEXUALLY ORIENTED MATTER INVOLVING A MINOR AND POSSESSION OF CRIMINAL TOOLS. ON JANUARY 31, 2006, THE DATE DEFENDANT-APPELLANT WAS SENTENCED, HE DID NOT RESIDE WITHIN 1,000 FEET OF A SCHOOL. THE SOUTH EUCLID-LYNDHURST BOARD OF EDUCATION DID NOT OWN THE PEDESTRIAN WALKWAY IN QUESTION AND THE ACTUAL SCHOOL PREMISES WAS MORE THAN 1,000 FEET FROM DEFENDANT-APPELLANT'S RESIDENCE. THE TRIAL COURT IMPROPERLY GRANTED THE CITY SUMMARY JUDGMENT AND ACTUALLY SHOULD HAVE GRANTED DEFENDANT-APPELLANT SUMMARY JUDGMENT.

The Standard of Review for an appellate court on a lower court's granting of summary judgment is "de novo" and the "de novo" review means that the appellate court uses the same standard that the trial court should have used, and the appellate court must examine the evidence to determine if, as a matter of law, no genuine issues exist for trial.

The issue in this matter is whether or not the trial court improperly granted appellee summary judgment. The standard of review for an appellate court on a lower court's granting of summary judgment is de novo. De novo review means that this court uses the same standard that the trial court should have used, and the court is to examine the evidence to determine whether, as a matter of law, no genuine issues exist for trial. Mitnaul v. Fairmount Presbyterian Church, (Cuyahoga Cty., 2002), 149 Ohio App. 3d 769, 2002-Ohio-5833, relying upon Brewer v. Cleveland City Schools, (Cuyahoga Cty., 1997), 122 Ohio App. 3d 378, which cited Dupler v. Mansfield Journal, (1980), 64 Ohio St. 2d 116. See also Renter, Admrx. v. Anthony, (Cuyahoga Cty., 2004), No. 82050, 2004-Ohio-465; and Lakatos v. Republic Waste Services of Ohio, (Cuyahoga Cty., 2004), No. 82256, 2004-Ohio-495. This court must review the lower court's granting of summary judgment de novo in accordance with the standards set forth in Rule 56 of the Ohio Rules of Civil Procedure. Moore v. City of Cleveland, (Cuyahoga Cty., 2004), No. 83070, 2004-Ohio-360.

Civ. R. 56(C) provides that before summary judgment may be granted, it must be determined that (1) no genuine issue as to any material fact remains to be litigated; (2) the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law; and (3) it appears from the evidence that reasonable minds can come to but one conclusion, and viewing such evidence most strongly in favor of the non-moving party, that conclusion is adverse to the party against whom the motion for summary judgment is made. State, ex rel. Parsons v. Fleming, (1994) 68 Ohio St. 3d 509, 511, citing Temple v. Wean United, Inc., (1977) 50 Ohio St. 2d 317. It is axiomatic that the three criteria must be met in order for the trial court to properly grant summary judgment. Summary judgment will not be granted until the movant sufficiently demonstrates the absence of any genuine issue of material fact. A "party seeking summary judgment, on the ground that the non-moving party cannot prove its case, bears the initial burden of informing the trial court of the basis for the motion, and identifying those portions of the record that demonstrate the absence of genuine issue of material fact on the essential element(s) of the non-moving party's claims." Dresher v. Burt, (1996) 75 Ohio St. 3d 280, 293. It is well settled that the party seeking summary judgment bears the burden of showing that no genuine issue of material fact exists for trial. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, (1987) 477 U.S. 317, 330, 106 S.Ct. 2548, 2556, 91 L.Ed. 2d 265, 278; Mitseff v. Wheeler, (1988) 38 Ohio St. 3d 112. Doubts must be resolved in favor of the non-moving party. Murphy v. Reynoldsburg, (1992) 65 Ohio St. 3d 356, 358-359. See also Payton v. Rehberg, (Cuyahoga County, 1997) 119 Ohio App. 3d 183. Appellant acknowledges, that she, as the non-moving party, must produce evidence on any issue for which she bears the burden of production at trial. Wing v. Anchor Media, Ltd. of Texas, (1991) 59 Ohio St. 3d 108, 111. In accordance with Civ. R. 56(B), Appellant, as the non-movant, may not rest upon the mere allegations or denial in her pleadings, but must set forth specific facts showing there is a genuine issue for trial. Chaney v. Clark Cty. Agricultural Soc., (Clark Cty., 1993) 90 Ohio App. 3d 421. In Dresher v. Burt, (1996) 75 Ohio St. 3d 280, the Supreme Court of Ohio modified the summary judgment standard as was applied under Wing, supra. Presently under the new standard, "the moving party" bears the initial responsibility of informing the trial court of the basis for the motion, and identifying those portions of the record which demonstrate the absence of a genuine issue of fact on a material element of the non-moving party's claim. Dresher at 296. The court must construe the evidence and all reasonable inferences drawn therefrom in a light most favorable to the party opposing the motion. Morris v. Ohio Cas. Ins. Co., (1988) 35 Ohio St. 3d 45. The motion must be overruled if reasonable minds could find for the party opposing for the motion. Saunders v. McFaul, (Cuyahoga Cty., 1990) 71 Ohio App. 3d 46.

Ohio Revised Code 2950.031(A) provides that no person who has pleaded guilty to a ". . . sexually oriented offense that is not a registration exempt sexually oriented offense shall . . . establish a residence or occupy a residential premises within 1,000 feet of any school premises. Ohio Revised Code 2950.01(R), the sexual predator statute, defines "school premises" as having the same meaning as in O.R.C. 2925.01. That section at (R) defines "school premises" as follows:

"School premises" means either of the following:

(1) the parcel of real property in which any school is situated, whether or not any instruction, extra curricular activities, or training provided by the school is being conducted on the premises at the time a criminal offense is committed;

(2) any other parcel of real property that is owned or leased by a Board of Education of a school, the governing authority of a community school established under Chapter 3314 of the Revised Code, or the governing body of a non-public school for which the State Board of Education prescribed minimum standards under Section 3301.07 of the Revised Code and on which some of the instruction, extra curricular activities or training of the school is conducted, whether or not any instruction, extra curricular activities, or training provided by the school is being conducted on the parcel of real property at the time a criminal offense is committed.

The factual issue presented in this matter is whether the South Euclid-Lyndhurst Board of Education owned a pedestrian walkway on January 31, 2006. The applicable facts demonstrate that in actuality the South Euclid-Lyndhurst Board of Education did not acquire ownership of the pedestrian walkway until August 2, 2006, almost six full months after plaintiff-appellee filed the complaint and sought the injunctions.

The South Euclid-Lyndhurst Board of Education owns the property known as Sunview School, Permanent Parcel No. 714-15-001, located at 5520 Meadow Woods Blvd., Lyndhurst, Ohio. See the affidavit of Jeffrey J. Filarski, the Lyndhurst City engineer, attached to plaintiff-appellee's motion for summary judgment. The Board has owned the property since 1960. However, as seen in the maps attached to Filarski's affidavit, and is referenced thereto, there had always been a 10-ft. wide pedestrian walkway, otherwise known as a pedestrian lane. The "school premises" prior to August 2, 2006, did not include the walkway. The legal description for the property did not include the 10-ft. pedestrian walkway. The walkway was owned by Albert B. Ratner and Sam M. Miller. See the affidavit of David A. Gembala, of Title Xperts Agency, Inc., attached to defendant-appellant's motion for summary judgment. The City of Lyndhurst, through its counsel, during the pendency of the litigation, unbeknownst to defendant-appellant, and without disclosure to the court, prepared a quit-claim deed, wherein as of July 26, 2006, Ratner and Miller transferred the 10-ft. wide walkway-lane to G & G Management Co., a partnership and the South Euclid-Lyndhurst Board of Education, each obtaining a half interest. The certified deed was not filed until August 2, 2006. The walkway, which had its own legal description, and was a recognized separate parcel of real estate, was then merged into the permanent parcel number of 714-15-001. This, of course, did not occur until the quit-claim deed was filed. Obviously, and without ambiguity, the Board of Education did not own the walkway until it was acquired by transfer on August 2, 2006. Defendant-appellant rhetorically asks if the walkway was owned by the Board of Education, then why was a quit-claim deed prepared and filed transferring the property to the Board? Obviously, property that is already owned does not need to be transferred to the owner.

Additionally, the quit-claim deed purporting to transfer one-half of the walkway to the South Euclid-Lyndhurst Board of Education is defective and of no consequence, as a matter of law. The quit-claim deed attached as defendant-appellant's Exhibit "D" in his response to plaintiff-appellee's motion for summary judgment clearly demonstrates, on its face, that Albert B. Ratner and Sam M. Miller transferred one-half of the walkway to G & G Management Co. and one-half to the South Euclid-Lyndhurst Board of Education. However, their marital status, as grantors, is undisclosed. The deed does not reflect if either of the individuals is married or single. O.R.C. 2103.02 provides for dower rights in Ohio. They entitle a spouse to an estate for life in one-third of the real property of which the spouse was seized as an estate of inheritance at any time during the marriage. Recitation in the deed of the grantor's marital status informs the grantee whether inchoate dower exists and aids others in establishing whether the grantee is receiving a clear title. This was completely absent. Additionally, a Board of Education of a city school district may acquire land by gift, pursuant to O.R.C. 3313.37(B)(1). However, the City in this matter has never demonstrated that the Board of Education ever adopted a resolution accepting the gift of the property. The Board cannot appropriately receive the property without a proper resolution. The purported transfer is defective.

Defendant-appellant maintains that the South Euclid-Lyndhurst Board of Education has not lawfully become the deeded owner of the disputed walkway. Regardless, prior to August 2, 2006, when the Board attempted to acquire the pedestrian walkway-lane, defendant-appellant did not reside within 1,000 feet of the "school premises". Defendant-appellant did not reside within 1,000 feet of the school premises until August 2, 2006, when the Board allegedly obtained the quit-claim deed for a one-half interest in the 10-ft. wide walkway. Defendant-appellant, in his response to plaintiff's motion for summary judgment clearly demonstrated that he resided 1,016.07 feet from the school premises. The School Board never maintained the walkway, did not insure it, and did not control the walkway prior to the acquisition in August of 2006. See defendant-appellant's previously referenced affidavit.

The trial court, in its final findings of fact, held, "Further still, the court finds that the pedestrian walkway is traversed by children going to and coming from school". The trial court had no basis upon which to formulate this finding of fact and impermissibly relied upon an assertion by the City in derogation of Civ. R. 56(A) and (E). On page 9 of the City's motion for summary judgment, plaintiff submitted that children use the pedestrian lane. There was no affidavit nor supporting evidence for the allegation. Civ. R. 56 requires an affidavit. There was no support for the allegation and it should not have been considered. Plaintiff-appellee merely attempted to improperly inflame the trial court with assertions of fact not appropriately supported by the requisite material demanded by Civ. R. 56. Again, the trial court again improperly granted summary judgment.

II. THE TRIAL COURT IMPROPERLY APPLIED O.R.C. 2950.031 TO DEFENDANT-APPELLANT IN THIS MATTER AND ITS APPLICATION CONSTITUTES AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL IMPINGEMENT UPON DEFENDANT-APPELLANT'S EXERCISE OF HIS PROPERTY RIGHTS SPECIFICALLY GRANTED BY THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND, THEREFORE, IS UNENFORCEABLE BOTH UNDER THE OHIO AND UNITED STATES CONSTITUTIONS.

The trial court erred in finding that O.R.C. 2950.031 does not violate the ex post facto clause as applied to defendant-appellant. The section is a criminal statute imposing criminal punishment and, therefore, violated the ex post facto clause. Additionally, in the event it is determined that the section is "civil", it is punitive in effect and, therefore, again violates the ex post facto clause. The application of the statute to this particular defendant-appellant further constitutes an unconstitutional Bill of Attainder and is impermissible. The trial court further erred by not engaging in a proper balancing of the equities under Section 2950.031.

Defendant Appellant is a 55-year-old married man with two emancipated children, who had no prior convictions for any crime. Defendant-appellant has resided with his wife at 5496 Lansbury Lane, Lyndhurst, Ohio, 44124, since November 28, 1979. The prohibition against residing near a school premises under O.R.C. 2950.031 did not come into effect until July 31, 2003, twenty-four years after defendant-appellant resided in his home. Further, as demonstrated above, on the date of sentencing, the South Euclid-Lyndhurst Board of Education did not own the walkway and defendant-appellant did not reside within 1,000 feet of the school. The retroactive application of the statute to this defendant is an unconstitutional impingement upon his exercise of his property rights, guaranteed to defendant-appellant as all other citizens of Ohio. The Ohio Constitution, Article I, Section 1, provides that all men are, by nature, free and independent and have certain inalienable rights among which are those of enjoying and defending life and liberty, acquiring, possessing, and protecting property, and seeking obtaining happiness and safety. The property rights are defined in O.R.C. 9.82. Defendant-appellant's property right is a "substantial right". Chef Italiano Corp. v. Kent State University, (1989), 44 Ohio St. 3d 86 at 88. A "substantial right" is defined as a right that under the United States Constitution, the Ohio Constitution, a statute, the common law, or a rule of procedure which entitles a person to enforce or protect. O.R.C. 2505.02(A)(1). Todd Dev. Co., Inc. v. Morgan, (Warren Cty., 2006), 2006-Ohio-4825. Defendant-appellant submits that the retroactive application of O.R.C. 2950.031, under these facts, as applied to him, would violate his substantial right to the property right protected by the Constitutions. Article II, Section 28 of the Ohio Constitution provides that "the General Assembly shall have no power to pass retroactive laws. Retroactive laws . . . have received the near universal distrust of civilization". Van Fossen, et al. v. Babcock & Wilcox Co., (1988) 36 Ohio St. 3d 100. This constitutional provision was intended to be more inclusive and more potent a protection than a prohibition against ex post facto laws only. See State v. Walls, (2002) 96 Ohio St. 3d 437, 2002-Ohio-509, at Par. 20, FN 4. One of the primary purposes of the retroactivity clause at Section 28, Article II of the Ohio Constitution, is to prevent the legislature from invading or interfering with the "vested rights" of individuals. See Bielat v. Bielat, (2000) 87 Ohio St. 3d at 357. A "vested right" may be created by common law or statute and is generally understood to be the power to lawfully do certain actions or possess certain things; in essence, it is a property right. Washington Cty. Taxpayers Assn. v. Pepel, (Washington Cty., 1992), 78 Ohio App. 3d 146. See also Wilson v. AC & S, Inc., (Butler Cty., 2006), 2006-Ohio-6704. The right to acquire property and live in it is an important right protected by the Ohio Constitution. Norwood v. Horney, (2006) 110 Ohio St. 3d 353, 2006-Ohio-3799, at P38. The court held that Ohio has always considered the right of property to be a fundamental right which should be strongly protected and must be trod upon lightly, no matter how great the weight of other forces.

III.  THE TRIAL COURT'S RETROACTIVE APPLICATION OF O.R.C. 2950.031 AS APPLIED TO THIS DEFENDANT-APPELLANT, VIOLATED HIS CONSTITUTIONAL DUE PROCESS RIGHTS AND, THEREFORE, IS UNENFORCEABLE.

The retroactive application of O.R.C. 2950.031 would violate defendant's due process rights because it would fail to give him fair notice of where, as a registered sex offender, he could live without being continually at risk of being ejected. When defendant-appellant was convicted, the Board of Education did not and arguably still does not, own the property in question. Accordingly, besides not being within 1,000 feet of the school premises, as of August, 2006, when the property was allegedly obtained by the Board, the Board basically "moved to the nuisance". In other words, the school moved within 1,000 feet of defendant-appellant. This application again would violate his due process rights according to the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution. How can defendant-appellant ever be safe from continual ejection?

IV.  THE TRIAL COURT ERRED BY APPLYING O.R.C. 2950.031 RETROACTIVELY IN VIOLATION OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND O.R.C. 1.48.

Initially, an appellate court considers an appeal from a trial court's interpretation and application of a statute de novo. State v. Sufrunko, (4th App. Dist., 1995), 105 Ohio App. 3d 504. A de novo requires an independent review of the trial court's decision without any deference to the trial court's determination. Brown v. Scioto Comm'rs., (4th App. Dist., 1993), 87 Ohio App., 3d 704; State v. Consilio, (9th Dist., 2006), 2006-Ohio-649 at P4. Accordingly, this court should not defer to the trial court's legal conclusions and this court should make its own interpretation of O.R.C. 1.48 regarding retroactivity of statutes, the Ohio Constitutional prohibition against retroactive laws, and the ex post facto clause as applied in O.R.C. 2950.031. In Ohio, statutes are presumed to be prospective unless specifically made retroactive. See O.R.C. 1.48 and State v. Consilio, supra. Under Article II, Section 28 of the Ohio Constitution, a statute is unconstitutional if it is retroactive and substantive rather than remedial. State v. LaSalle, (2002) 92 Ohio St. 3d 178, 2002-Ohio-4009. The court in LaSalle, adopted a two-part test to determine whether a statute is unconstitutionally retroactive. First, the court must determine whether the legislature actually intended the statute to be applied retroactively. Second, if the court determines that the legislature intended the statute to apply retroactively, then and only then must the court determine whether the statute is substantive or remedial. State v. Cook, (1998) 83 Ohio St. 3d 404, 1998-Ohio-291. There is no language in O.R.C. 2950.031 that explicitly references retroactivity nor dates of conviction for the offenders to which the section applies. Therefore, as did the court in State v. Consilio, supra., this court should conclude that the legislature intended for O.R.C. 2950.031 to apply prospectively only. Accordingly, because the Board of Education did not acquire the property until after defendant-appellant's conviction, the statute cannot permissibly apply to defendant-appellant and the trial court should be reversed.

In the event this Honorable Court determines that O.R.C. 2950.031 is intended to apply retroactively, it would violate the Ohio Constitutional prohibition against retroactive laws, as applied to defendant-appellant. The Ohio Constitution prohibits retroactive laws that directly impair substantive rights. Defendant-appellant concedes that it does not prohibit remedial measures that incidentally affect those rights. See State, ex rel. Matz v. Brown, (1988) 37 Ohio St. 3d 279. Substantive law is that which creates duties, rights and obligations while procedural or remedial law prescribes methods of enforcement of rights or of obtaining redress. Kunkler v. Goodyear Tire & Rubber Co., (1988) 36 Ohio St. 3d 135. Irrespective of O.R.C. 2950.031, defendant-appellant, a law abiding citizen, has a very precious and sacrosanct right to reside in his own home with his wife. Without committing any new crime, the trial court denied defendant-appellant of his right. There can be no cogent argument that the statute is "remedial". The statute as applied to defendant-appellant makes absolutely no sense whatsoever. The trial court precluded defendant-appellant from residing within 1,000 feet of a school. However, children are everywhere. The court did not prevent defendant-appellant from living in a building with children, near a park, hospital, playground, or any other area where children congregate. In fact, defendant-appellant is even permitted to visit a school. There is nothing preventing defendant-appellant from visiting his wife at their home during the day, arguably even when school is in session. This court should not accept a simplistic assertion that O.R.C. 2950.031 is a remedial measure with only an incidental effect upon defendant-appellant. Rather, and markedly different than merely a registration requirement under Chapter 2950, this court should conclude that O.R.C. 2950.031, as applied to defendant-appellant, improperly violated the Ohio Constitution prohibition against retroactive laws and represents a severe onerous hardship on defendant-appellant.

V.  THE TRIAL COURT'S APPLICATION OF O.R.C. 2950.031 TO DEFENDANT-APPELLANT VIOLATES THE CONSTITUTIONAL PROTECTION AGAINST EX POST FACTO LAWS.

The United States Constitution prohibits State governments from passing ex post facto laws. Article I, Section 10, CL. 1. Any statute which makes more burdensome the punishment for a crime, after its commission, is prohibited as ex post facto. Beazell v. Ohio, (1925) 269 U.S. 167, 46 S.Ct. 68, 70, L.Ed.. 216. This court must first determine whether O.R.C. 2950.031 imposes a retroactive criminal punishment or whether it is merely a "civil proceedings". Smith v. Doe, (2003) 538 U.S. 84, 123 S.Ct. 1140, 155 L.Ed. 2d 164. If the law is clearly criminal, then the analysis is completed and the law should be found unconstitutional. If, however, the legislature denominates the statute as civil, the court must determine whether the law is nonetheless so punitive either in purpose or in effect as to negate the State's intention to deem it civil. Smith v. Doe, supra. Defendant-appellant submits herein that O.R.C. 2950.031 is a criminal statute imposing criminal punishment and, therefore, violates the ex post facto clause. When defendant-appellant was sentenced, he was not informed that eviction from his home would occur as part of his sentence. Regardless, the ex post facto prohibition encompasses punitive conditions outside the sentence. State v. Ahedo, (Cuyahoga Cty., 1984), 14 Ohio App.3d 254, citing Weaver v. Graham, (1981) 450 U.S. 24, 101 S.Ct. 960, 67 L.Ed. 2d 17; and "subtle ex post facto violations are no more permissible than overt ones". Collins v. Youngblood, (1990) 497 U.S. 37, 110 S.Ct. 2715, 111 L.Ed. 2d 30. O.R.C. 2950.031 is part of Chapter 2950 and part of the Criminal Chapter of the Ohio Revised Code. Chapter 2950 specifically deals with sexual offenders. There is nothing in O.R.C. 2950.031 that specifically references the purpose of or justification for the residency restriction. The statute itself is silent as to whether it is a criminal or civil statute. Accordingly, this court should not accept a simplistic assertion that O.R.C. 2950.031 is a civil statute. Defendant-appellant is being sanctioned for merely being a convicted sex offender. The trial court did not make any determination of present or future dangerousness or consideration of any fairness and the prohibition is clearly similar to a criminal punishment than a civil regulation. Defendant-appellant submits that this court should make a specific finding that O.R.C. 2950.031 is explicitly criminal and, therefore, violates the ex post facto clause as applied to him. Defendant-appellant did not live within 1,000 feet of a school at the time of his sentence.

Even if this Honorable Court determines that O.R.C. 2950.031 is civil, it is punitive in effect and, therefore, again violates the ex post facto clause. A statute can be so punitive in effect that it overcomes the legislature label. Smith v. Doe, supra. The court must consider whether the statute inflicts criminal punishment or merely institutes a civil remedial scheme. The considerations are as set forth in Smith v. Doe, supra.: (1) whether the sanction involves an affirmative disability or restraint; (2) whether it has historically been regarded as punishment; (3) whether its operation will promote the traditional aims of punishment; (4) whether an alternative purpose to which it may rationally be connected is assigned for it; and (5) whether it appears excessive in relation to the alternative purpose assigned. An analysis of the factors lead to one conclusion; O.R.C. 2950.031 is punitive in effect and cannot be applied retroactively to defendant-appellant who did not reside within 1,000 feet of a school at the time of his sentence. The statute clearly has a retributive and deterrent effect and imposes an affirmative restraint upon a substantial right protected by the Ohio Constitution. There is no evidence whatsoever that the statute is intended to protect the safety and general welfare of citizens. If so, the statute is completely nonsensical. A defendant would not be precluded from interacting with children merely by not living within 1,000 feet of a school. The statute is similar to a banishment or parole. The statute is not reasonably related to a legislative goal of decreasing the risk of sex offenses against minors. In this matter, defendant-appellant merely downloaded inappropriate images. There was never an allegation of attempting to communicate with a child. Clearly, under the ex post facto clause, a State is prohibited from enacting a law that increases a punishment for a criminal act after the act has been committed. This is precisely what has happened in this matter. He did not live within 1,000 feet of a school at the time of his conviction.

Defendant-appellant submits that this entire matter is controlled by Nasal v. Dover, (Miami Cty., 2006), 2006-Ohio-5584. In Nasal, the State filed a petition seeking to enjoin a registered sex offender from residing within 1,000 feet of an elementary school pursuant to O.R.C. 2950.031. The Court of Appeals held that the statute prohibiting a registered sex offender from residing within 1,000 feet of any school premises was unconstitutionally retroactive as applied to the offender. The court held that the statute affected the registered sex offender's substantive right to maintain his residence and was, therefore, unconstitutionally retroactive as applied to him. As in this matter, the offender owned and occupied his home near the school for several years prior to the enactment of the statute under which the trial court entered the injunctive order requiring him to vacate the residence. This violated the Ohio Constitution, Article II, Section 28; O.R.C. 1.48 and 2950.031. The court considered the recent Ohio Supreme Court decision in Smith v. Smith, (2006) 109 Ohio St. 3d 285, 2006-Ohio-2419, wherein the court stated that Article II, Section 28 of the Ohio Constitution prohibits the General Assembly from passing retroactive laws and protects vested rights from new legislative encroachments. The retroactivity clause nullifies those new laws that reach back and create new burdens, new duties, new obligations, or new liabilities not existing at the time the statute becomes effective. To determine whether a law is unconstitutionally retroactive, the court must first determine whether the General Assembly expressly intended the statute to apply retroactively. If so, the court must determine whether the statute is substantive rendering it unconstitutionally retroactive as opposed to merely remedial. The court in Nasal further again reiterated that statutes are presumed to apply only prospectively unless specifically made retroactive. O.R.C. 1.48. The court in Nasal interpreted O.R.C. 2950.031 and determined that the statute, although enacted after the defendant's conviction, was intended by the General Assembly to prohibit someone in the defendant's situation from continuing to occupy a residence located within 1,000 feet of the school. Accordingly, in Nasal, the court concluded that O.R.C. 2950.031 was intended to apply retroactively. The court then considered whether it affected a substantive right or was merely remedial. The court again focused upon the right of property to be a fundamental right. The court found that the defendant owned and occupied his current residence before he was convicted of a sexual offense. The court concluded that the statute would require him to leave his home, at least, if not also to divest himself of ownership of his home. The court further concluded that this affected a substantive right specifically, the defendant's right to maintain the residence he owned and in which he resided for years prior to the enactment of O.R.C. 2950.031. Accordingly, the court found that the residency restriction of O.R.C. 2950.031 as applied to the facts of the Nasal case, constituted a retroactive law affecting a substantive right. The court concluded that the statute was unconstitutional when it was applied to require an owner and occupier of real estate, who owned and occupied the real estate before the enactment of the statute, to vacate the residence. Defendant herein respectfully submits that the analysis in Nasal should be and must be adopted herein.

VI.  THE TRIAL COURT ERRED BY FAILING TO MEANINGFULLY ENGAGE IN A BALANCING OF THE EQUITIES UNDER O.R.C. 2950.031.

The trial court erred by failing to engage in a balancing of the-equities under O.R.C. 2950.031. The court in its conclusions of law, stated "further, this court is mindful of the public policy concerns regarding public safety. The court also recognizes the basic right to own property and, therefore, must balance these two important issues in rendering this opinion. However, the court in actuality did not consider the public policy concerns regarding public safety. As previously stated, there is no safety gained whatsoever by the residency prohibition. A sexual offender, who intends to re-offend, could certainly have ample access to children without living within 1,000 feet of a school. The public safety issue is preposterous because the statute does not further the goal. The court recognizes the basic right to own property but gave the substantial right short shrift and no consideration. The legislature when enacting O.R.C. 2950.031, at paragraph (B), removed the element that appellee's burden of proof is to show irreparable injury. Otherwise, the statute is the same as when anyone else seeks injunctive relief. The trial court is required to balance the equities in this case as with any other injunctive relief sought. Zavakos v. Zavakos Enterprises, Inc., et al., (2nd Dist., 1989), 63 Ohio App. 3d 100.

VII.  O.R.C. 2950.031 AS APPLIED TO DEFENDANT-APPELLANT HEREIN DEMONSTRATES A BILL OF ATTAINDER PROHIBITED BY ARTICLE I, SECTION 9 AND 10 TO THE FEDERAL CONSTITUTION.

A Bill of Attainder is a law that (1) inflicts punishment; (2) without a judicial trial; and (3) upon an identifiable individual. The United States Constitution, Article I, Section 10; State v. Williams, (2000) 88 Ohio St. 3d 513. Defendant-appellant concedes that in State v. Ward, (Cuyahoga Cty., 1999), 130 Ohio App. 3d 551, the court held that the registration and notification provisions of the sexual predator law were not unconstitutional Bill of Attainders. See State v. Williams, supra. However, in Williams, the decision was based upon a conclusion that the registration and requirements were merely remedial measures which did not constitute criminal punishment. A statute which precludes an individual from maintaining his residence of several decades is completely different and is completely punitive as discussed above. The residence statute as applied to this defendant is clearly a Bill of Attainder which inflicts punishment and is unconstitutional.

CONCLUSION

The trial court erred in granting the City of Lyndhurst summary judgment. The South Euclid-Lyndhurst Board of Education did not own the disputed walkway at the time of defendant-appellant's conviction. If the Board acquired the property, the "nuisance" was located to the defendant-appellant. An application of O.R.C. 2950.031 would be an unconstitutional impairment of defendant-appellant's rights. The trial court's order should be reversed and the injunction dissolved.
