II. LAW AND ARGUMENT.
This Court should deny OTARMA and National Casualty's summary judgment motions for three reasons. First, this Court (or, more-aptly, the fact-finder in the underlying Gillispie Lawsuit) must resolve genuine issues of material fact before judgment can issue in favor of OTARMA and National Casualty. Second, per the plain language of the OTARMA and National Casualty policies, the conduct that Mr. Gillispie alleges in the Gillispie Lawsuit, if proven, will trigger coverage because that conduct and the resulting harm and injuries occurred during and throughout the applicable coverage periods. Finally, because the coverage questions in this case are matters of first impression in this state, this Court should establish precedent that adequately addresses the complex and continuing nature of the harm and injuries present in wrongful conviction litigation. That precedent should be application of the continuous trigger theory, which will trigger coverage under the OTARMA policies and the National Casualty policy.
A. GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT REMAIN WHICH PREVENT SUMMARY JUDGMENT IN FAVOR OF OTARMA AND NATIONAL CASUALTY.
Pursuant to Civ.R. 56(C), summary judgment is appropriate if (1) no genuine issue of material fact remains to be litigated; (2) the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law; and (3) viewing the evidence most strongly in favor of the non-moving party, reasonable minds can come to but one conclusion, and that conclusion is adverse to the party against whom

the motion for summary judgment is made. See State ex rel. Zimmerman v. Tompkins 75 Ohio St. 3d 447, 448 (1996). When a motion for summary judgment is made and supported as provided in Civ.R. 56, the non-moving party may not rest on the mere allegations of its pleading; rather, its response must set forth specific facts showing that there is a genuine triable issue. See Civ. R. 56(E); Jackson v. Alert Fire & Safety Equip., Inc., 58 Ohio St. 3d 48, 52 (1991). In this case, several questions of material fact remain which should prevent this Court from granting summary judgment in favor of OTARMA and National Casualty.
The Gillispie Lawsuit alleges that Mr. Gillispie was wrongfully arrested, prosecuted, and imprisoned in 1991 because of wrongful conduct by the Miami Township Defendants. (See Ex. 1, 11 76-125). The Gillispie Lawsuit is still pending in the United States District Court for the Southern District of Ohio and includes Section 1983 and related State law claims (such as malicious prosecution). In Selective Ins. Co. v. RLI Ins. Co., 706 F.App'x 260 (6th Cir. 2017), the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals noted that:
Under Ohio law, malicious prosecution requires the 'instituting' or 'continuing' of prosecution without probable cause. . . . In other words, a defendant can recover for a prosecution that was not malicious at its inception, but only became malicious later, when it continued without probable cause. The key issue is whether there was probable cause, and ... when any probable cause disappeared. The time when probable cause disappeared can also determine when a § 1983 deprivation-of-due-process claim occurs, because continued custody and prosecution without probable cause is a violation of constitutional rights.
Id. at 266 (internal citations omitted). That question - when a prosecution turns "malicious" for want of probable case - is a question of fact. See id.
Before the Court can grant OTARMA and National Casualty's summary judgment motions and issue a declaration that they are not obligated to defend or indemnify the Miami Township Defendants, there must be a probable cause determination. That is a question of fact. Additionally,

because both OTARMA's and National Casualty's policies include coverage carve-outs for intentional or otherwise fraudulent conduct, a determination whether the alleged conduct of the Miami Township Defendants was intentional or negligent must also occur. Lastly, an issue of fact remains as to when and under what circumstances exculpatory evidence was withheld from Mr. Gillispie. Determination of these unresolved facts is critical to determining the applicability or non-applicability of the policy provisions that OTARMA and National Casualty rely on in support of their arguments.
These facts are being litigated in the Gillispie Lawsuit. Until resolved, summary judgment in favor of OTARMA and National Casualty, or for that matter, in favor of the Miami Township Defendants on their cross-motion for summary judgment, is inappropriate. This Court should stay further proceedings in this declaratory judgment action, or dismiss the complaints for declaratory judgment without prejudice, pending the conclusion of the Gillispie Lawsuit.
If the Court is not persuaded to stay further proceedings or to dismiss the complaints without prejudice, pending the conclusion of the Gillispie Lawsuit, the Court should overrule the motions for summary judgment filed by OTARMA and National Casualty and grant the cross- motion for summary filed by the Miami Township Defendants for the reasons set forth in the remainder of this memorandum and the cross-motion for summary judgment.
B. UNDER THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF THE GLC AGREEMENT AND THE LDCPA, OTARMA IS OBLIGATED TO DEFEND AND INDEMNIFY THE MIAMI TOWNSHIP DEFENDANTS IN THE GILLISPIE LAWSUIT.
The GLC Agreement and LDCPA are "occurrence" policies. For coverage to be triggered, a triggering event must occur during the coverage period. Under the plain language of the GLC Agreement and LDCPA, OTARMA has a duty to defend and indemnify the Miami Township Defendants in the Gillispie Lawsuit.

In its Motion for Summary Judgment, OTARMA correctly points out that although OTARMA is not a traditional insurance company (rather, it is a joint self-insurance pool created per R.C. 2744.081), this Court can consider well-settled principles of insurance law when considering OTARMA's obligations and duties under the GLC Agreement and LDCPA. See Ohio Gov't. Risk Mgt. Plan v. County Risk Sharing Authority, 130 Ohio App.3d 174, 719 N.E.2d 992 (6th Dist. 1998). One principle is that defense or indemnity obligations are determined by construing the terms of the coverage documents with the allegations in the underlying lawsuit. See e.g., Ohio Gov't Risk Plan v. Harrison, 115 Ohio St.3d 241, 2007-Ohio-4948, 874 N.E.2d 1155, 1 19; J.C. Penney Cas. Ins. Co. v. Professionals Ins. Co. of Ohio, 67 Ohio App.3d 167, 174, 586 N.E.2d 222 (1990). Reviewing the allegations in the Gillispie Lawsuit and comparing them with the plain language of the GLC Agreement and LDCPA, OTARMA is obligated to defend and indemnify the Miami Township Defendants. Accordingly, its Motion for Summary Judgment should be denied.
i. Mr. Gillispie alleges qualifying "injuries" under the GLC Agreement and LDCPA.
First, Mr. Gillispie alleges a "personal injury" under the GLC Agreement - Government Law Enforcement Liability Coverage Part which, as defined, includes malicious prosecution, mental injury, anguish or shock, humiliation, and violations of rights protected by civil rights statutes. (See Ex. 3, at Government Law Enforcement Liability Coverage Part, p. 2). The LDCPA defines an "injury" as "harm arising out of ... mental injury, anguish, shock or humiliation .. . [or] improper detention, false arrest, false imprisonment, malicious prosecution . . . [or a] violation of rights as protected by civil rights statutes." (See Ex. 4, p. 3). The Gillispie Lawsuit alleges destruction of evidence, suggestive identification, fabrication of evidence, as well as Section 1983 and related state law claims. (See Ex. 1, 11 76-127). The Gillispie Lawsuit also contains a cause of

action for intentional infliction of emotional distress, qualifying as "mental injury, anguish, shock or humiliation." (See Ex. 1, 11 117-121). On its face, the Gillispie Complaint plainly alleges a qualifying "injury" under the GLC Agreement and LDCPA.
ii. Mr. Gillispie's alleged "injuries" were caused by "occurrences" under the GLC Agreement and LDCPA.
Mr. Gillispie's "injuries" were also caused by "occurrences" under the plain language of the GLC Agreement and LDCPA. In the GLC Agreement - Government Law Enforcement Liability Coverage Part, an "occurrence" means an "incident brought about by law enforcement action committed or alleged to have been committed. (See Ex. 3 at Government Law Enforcement Liability Coverage Part, p. 2)." Without any question, the allegations of official misconduct in the Gillispie Lawsuit meet the GLC Agreement's broad definition of "occurrence."
The same holds true under the LDCPA, which defines an "occurrence" as "an accident including continuous or repeated exposure to substantially the same general harmful conditions, event, happening or a commission of a wrongful act." (See Ex. 4, p. 3). A "wrongful act" is defined as "any actual or alleged error, misstatement, act of omission, neglect or breach of duty, including malfeasance, misfeasance or nonfeasance, but excluding willful misconduct, criminal conduct, fraud or malicious acts." (See Ex. 4, p. 4). The term "accident" is undefined. When construing undefined words in an insurance policy, a court must give them their plain and ordinary meaning. See e.g., Prudential Property & Cas. Ins. Co. v. Koby, 124 Ohio App.3d 174, 177 (11th Dist. 1997). The plain and ordinary meaning of "accident" is "an unforeseen and unplanned event or circumstance" or "an unfortunate event resulting especially from carelessness or ignorance." Merriam-Webster's Online Dictionary and Thesaurus, https://www.merriam-webster.com/ dictionary /accident (accessed February 18, 2019).

Applying the LDCPA's defined term "wrongful act" and the plain and ordinary meaning of "accident," Mr. Gillispie alleges multiple and continuing trigger occurrences under the LDCPA, including errors, misstatements, acts of omission, breaches of duty, malfeasance and nonfeasance by the Miami Township Defendants. Specifically:
Suppressing exculpatory evidence;
Suggestive identification;
Fabricated evidence;
Malicious prosecution;
Destruction of exculpatory evidence; and
Spoliation of evidence.
(See Ex. 1, 11 76-125). Each of the aforementioned allegations and causes of action constitute "wrongful acts" sufficient to qualify as multiple and continuing "occurrences." Similarly, using the plain and ordinary meaning of the undefined term "accident" to mean "an unforeseen and unplanned event or circumstance" or "an unfortunate event resulting especially from carelessness or ignorance," coverage is triggered under the LDCPA. Importantly, while "willful misconduct, criminal conduct, fraud or malicious act" are excluded from LDCPA coverage, there are no facts before this Court which would establish that the exclusion should apply.6
iii. Mr. Gillispie alleges an "occurrence" within the applicable policy periods.
For coverage under the OTARMA policies to be triggered, occurrence(s) thereunder must have taken place on or after May 28, 1994 for Wrongful Acts Coverage, or on or after July 24, 1996 for Law Enforcement Operations Coverage and Third Party Liability Coverage. Mr. Gillispie alleges multiple and continuing occurrences ranging from suppression and spoliation of evidence

D. WHEN THERE ARE ALLEGATIONS OF CONTINUOUS WRONGFUL CONDUCT, COVERAGE SHOULD BE TRIGGERED UNDER ANY POLICY IN EFFECT DURING THE PERIOD SUCH CONTINUOUS WRONGFUL CONDUCT OCCURRED.
If this Court is unpersuaded that the plain and unambiguous language of the policies demonstrate OTARMA and National Casualty's coverage obligations, it must turn its attention to a question of law that, to date, has not been addressed in this state; that is, when is insurance coverage triggered in wrongful conviction litigation? Because Section 1983 claims - particularly Brady violations - often involve individuals who are in prison for many years, disputes arise among insurers as to which insurance policies are triggered. To adjudicate these disputes, courts have developed multiple "trigger" theories:
The "Arrest-Conviction Rule" provides that coverage is triggered only under a policy in effect on the date of arrest and/or conviction;
* The "Exoneration Rule" provides that coverage is triggered only under the policy in effect on the date of exoneration; and
* The "Continuous Trigger Rule" or "Multiple Trigger Rule" provides that coverage is triggered under all applicable policies in effect between arrest through exoneration.
7 As reflected in Ex. 2, Mr. Gillispie filed a post-conviction pleading during the National Casualty policy's coverage period.

Under either the Arrest-Conviction Rule or Exoneration Rule, only a single year's policy is triggered. OTARMA and National Casualty advocate for application of the "Arrest-Conviction Rule" in this case:
The trigger date for coverage of claims involving section 1983 claims, including Brady violations, under an occurrence-based policy is the date when the tort occurred. As stated by the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals in a similar case, '[t]he clear majority of courts have held that the tort occurs when the underlying criminal charges are filed.'
(See OTARMA Motion, p. 16, citing Genesis Ins. Co. v. City of Council Bluffs, 667 F.3d 806, 812- 13 (8th Cir. 2012) and City of Erie, PA v. Guar. Nat'l Ins. Co., 109 F.3d 156, 159-60 (3d Cir. 1997)). Because the OTARMA policies are retroactive - at the earliest - to May 28, 1994, and because the National Casualty policy was only in effect from July 31, 1995 to July 31, 1996, OTARMA and National Casualty argue that conduct occurring in 1991 does not trigger coverage under their policies. What they do not consider, however, is that the allegedly wrongful conduct did not stop once Mr. Gillispie was convicted. Countless opportunities arose for information about exculpatory evidence to be divulged. Each time that information was withheld, a new harm and new injury manifested itself.
The Miami Township Defendants advocate for the Continuous Trigger Rule. Application of the Arrest-Conviction Rule will establish precedent that would leave Miami Township and many other local governments uninsured in cases like the Gillispie Lawsuit. Doing so will burden already debt-laden communities and potentially deprive exonerated persons of adequate redress for meritorious claims.

i. Application of the Continuous Trigger Rule is proper where the alleged wrongdoing - particularly Brady violations - is continuous and ongoing over an extended period.
Section 1983 claims are manifested in a variety of forms, ranging from suppression of exculpatory evidence to malicious prosecution. No Ohio court has resolved the question of when, for insurance purposes, coverage should be triggered in those cases. Other courts generally use either the time of arrest/conviction or the time of exoneration as the potential periods for triggering insurance coverage in cases like the Gillispie Lawsuit. The problem with analyzing Section 1983 claims alleging Brady violations using these two isolated moments in time is that they ignore the continuous, ongoing, and repetitive nature of Brady violations.
OTARMA and National Casualty rely heavily on the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals recent decision in Selective Ins. Co. v. RLI Ins. Co., 706 F.App'x 260 (6th Cir. 2017). (See OTARMA Motion, pp. 16-17; National Casualty Motion, pp. 12-14). In Selective Insurance, the Sixth Circuit concluded that under an occurrence-based policy, an excess insurer was not liable for paying a claim arising out of wrongful conviction litigation because coverage was triggered outside the policy period. The Court used the moment probable cause was lost in the underlying police investigation as the trigger point for coverage:
Under Ohio law, malicious prosecution requires the 'instituting' or 'continuing' of prosecution without probable cause. *** In other words, a defendant can recover for a prosecution that was not malicious at its inception, but only became malicious later, when it continued without probable cause. The key issue is whether there was probable cause, and ... when any probable cause disappeared. The time when probable cause disappeared can also determine when a § 1983 deprivation-of-due-process claim occurs, because continued custody and prosecution without probable cause is a violation of constitutional rights.
Selective Ins. Co., 706 F.App'x 260, 266 (6th Cir. 2017) (internal citations omitted). With its focus on the timing of probable cause, the Court reversed a decision of the United States District Court

for the Northern District of Ohio, which had predicted that Ohio would follow the Arrest- Conviction Rule.
OTARMA and National Casualty's reliance on Selective Insurance is misguided for two reasons. First, the Sixth Circuit did not adopt the Arrest-Conviction Rule, nor did it predict that Ohio would follow the Arrest-Conviction Rule. Second, the Court did not address the question presented here, to wit: whether allegations of continuing misconduct constitute multiple or continuous triggers. In Selective Insurance, the prosecution of the wrongfully-convicted man in the underlying case began legitimately and became malicious or wrongful only after exculpatory evidence was withheld. At that time, probable cause was lost, the prosecution turned malicious, and coverage was triggered. But because the prosecution was never wrongful or malicious during the excess carrier's policy period, the Court found no coverage. In contrast, Mr. Gillispie alleges that the Miami Township Defendants engaged in continuous and repeated misconduct, the effect of which kept him behind bars and which continued long after his 1991 conviction. (See Ex. 1, 1 73). Therefore, Selective Insurance, in the context it is relied upon by OTARMA and National Casualty, has limited precedential value.
In rebuttal, OTARMA and National Casualty will point out that the Continuous Trigger Rule has been historically disfavored in wrongful-conviction litigation. See e.g., Northfield Ins. Co. v. City of Waukegan, 761 F.Supp.2d 766, 773 (N.D.Ill.2010) (noting that, "despite the split in views on insurance coverage for civil rights claims" between cases advocating the Arrest- Conviction Rule and cases advocating the Exoneration Theory, "neither strain of cases endorses the continuing violation theory"). However, at least one court has recently bucked that trend, and, with increasing numbers of exonerations nationwide, more will follow suit. See Travelers Indemn. Co. v. Forrest Cty., 206 F. Supp. 3d 1216, 1224-1225 (S.D. Miss. 2016) (finding that "[t]he policy

here plainly provides coverage for injuries sustained during the policy period, and [the exonerated] plainly alleged that [they] were in prison for a crime they did not commit during the policy period. Therefore, [the exonerated] alleged an injury during the policy period") (appeal pending); see also Travelers Indemn. Co. v. Forrest Cty., S.D.Miss. No. 2:14-CV-22-KS-MTP, 2017 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 217767, at *19 (Mar. 30, 2017).
OTARMA and National Casualty's preference for application of the Arrest-Conviction Rule is unsurprising given the timeline of relevant events and retroactive dates of coverage in this case, but it is incongruent with the facts presented; that is, the Gillispie Lawsuit looks to hold the Miami Township Defendants liable for alleged continuing misconduct over many years and not just for alleged misconduct at the time of his conviction. (See Ex. 1, 1 73). OTARMA argues: In this case, Mr. Gillispie alleges in his Amended Complaint that the Miami Township Defendants' wrongful behavior occurred during their investigation of the rapes, their arrest of Mr. Gillispie, and ultimately their testimony at trial. Thus, according to the allegations, the Miami Township Defendants' behavior was wrongful as early as 1990, but certainly no later than Mr. Gillispie's conviction in June 1991.
(OTARMA Motion, p. 17) (emphasis added). In reality, Mr. Gillispie alleges that the Miami Township Defendants engaged in a continuous course of misconduct, the effect of which kept him behind bars and continued long after his 1991 conviction:
Accordingly, even after the conviction, [some of the Miami Township Defendants] have continued to work to keep Mr. Gillispie behind bars by repeating their lies and misrepresentations in post- conviction proceedings and through efforts to conceal their own misconduct, thereby causing Mr. Gillispie further damage.
(See Ex. 1, 1 73) (emphasis added). "The duty to defend is generally determined by construing the material terms of the insurance policy and the allegations in the pleadings in the action against the insured." J.C. Penney Cas. Ins. Co. v. Professionals Ins. Co., 67 Ohio App.3d 167, 174, 586 N.E.2d

222 (6th Dist.1990) citing Willoughby Hills v. Cincinnati Ins. Co. (1984), 9 Ohio St.3d 177, 9 OBR 463, 459 N.E.2d 555, syllabus. Here, the material terms of the policies and the allegations in the Gillispie Lawsuit demonstrate that OTARMA and National Casualty have duties of defense and indemnity.
This Court has been presented with a legal fiction - that the harm to Mr. Gillispie and the allegedly wrongful conduct of the Miami Township Defendants ended for insurance purposes when Mr. Gillispie was convicted in 1991. Not so. Mr. Gillispie's alleged harm was continuous for more than two decades and resurfaced every time he filed one of his post-conviction motions, appeals, or petitions. As such, any policies in place during that period when the wrongful conduct was continuing - 1991 through 2013 - ought to be triggered based on the plain and unambiguous language of the coverage documents.8 See Diocese of Winona v. Interstate Fire & Cas. Co., 841 F. Supp. 894, 898 (D. Minn. 1992) ("It is difficult to understand why a policy could not be triggered if continued negligence and injury were present in a policy issued after the first [incident of sexual abuse"]; Roman Catholic Diocese of Joliet v. Lee, 292 Ill. App. 3d 447, 685 N.E.2d 932, 938-39, 226 Ill. Dec. 477 (Ill. App. Ct. 1997) (same conclusion). OTARMA's policy meets that criteria, as does National Casualty's policy.
Mr. Gillispie's 1991 conviction is separate and distinct from his more than two decades of incarceration which, in turn, are separate and distinct from his claims of continuing deprivation of rights based upon the continuing withholding of exculpatory evidence. Accordingly, this Court should deny the summary judgment motions filed by OTARMA and National Casualty.

ii. Ohio courts have applied the Continuous Trigger Rule in other insurance contexts.
In support of the Arrest-Conviction Rule, OTARMA states that the "conclusion that the trigger date is the date of harm finds support in other areas of Ohio law." (See OTARMA Motion, p. 19). OTARMA cites to Lightning Rod Mut. Ins. Co. v. South Worth, 4th Dist. Scioto No. 15CA3704, 2016-Ohio-3473, 55 N.E.3d 1174. In that case, the Fourth District Court of Appeals found that coverage under an insurance policy did not exist because the damages alleged in the underlying lawsuit - negligent installation of a mobile home - were merely a continuation of the same damages that occurred before the applicable policy dates. See id. 11 34-35. Lightning Rod is clearly distinguishable in that the alleged negligent installation of a mobile home truly occurs only once - the time it is negligently installed. In contrast, exculpatory evidence allegedly was not withheld from Mr. Gillispie once; it was allegedly withheld multiple times such that the wrongful conduct continued for more than two decades.
A more-analogous situation is found in toxic exposure and environmental liability cases where the resulting harm and damages might not present for a significant period. In these circumstances, Ohio courts have adopted the Continuous Trigger Rule. See e.g., Goodyear Tire & Rubber Co. v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 95 Ohio St. 3d 512, 2002 Ohio 2842, 769 N.E.2d 835, paragraph one of the syllabus ("when a continuous occurrence of environmental pollution triggers claims under multiple primary insurance policies, the insured is entitled to secure coverage from a single policy of its choice that covers 'all sums' incurred as damages 'during the policy period,' subject to that policy's limit of coverage"); Polk v. Landings of Walden Condominium Assn., 11th Dist. Portage No. 2004-P-0075, 2005-Ohio-4042, 1 90 (applying the continuous trigger theory to find insurers liable for a property loss claim where mold and water intrusions were present during both insurers' policy periods and noting that "coverage is triggered so long as property damage

occurs during the policy period"); Hartford Ins. Co. v. Commercial Union Ins. Co. Erie App. No. E-79-6, 1979 Ohio App. LEXIS 9786 (June 15, 1979) (a property owner recovered a judgment against the city of Sandusky for damages his property sustained over the course of nearly 20 years as a result of "a continuing underground trespass of city water"). Even in the property damage context relied on by OTARMA, courts in this state have applied the continuous trigger doctrine. See Plum v. W. Am. Ins. Co., 1st Dist. Hamilton No. C-050115, 2006-Ohio-452, 1 16 (applying the continuous trigger theory in defective-build suit against home builder).
Suffice it to say, the Continuous Trigger Rule has significant support in analogous areas of Ohio law. Extending the Continuous Trigger Rule here is a logical next step.
iii. Applying the Continuous Trigger Rule is in the best interest of the State, its local governments, and the wrongfully-convicted.
Miami Township would be uninsured for what could be an extraordinary financial liability if OTARMA and National Casualty were to prevail in this lawsuit and if Mr. Gillispie were to prevail in his lawsuit against the Miami Township Defendants. The Continuous Trigger Rule, coupled with a focus on the plain language of applicable policies, will create an opportunity for courts to assign and apportion coverage obligations appropriately. For example, presume the following set of facts:
Man is convicted in 2020 and sentenced to 40 years in prison;
Insurer A issues law enforcement liability coverage for 1 policy year - 2020;
Insurer B, in 2021, issues law enforcement liability coverage that continues unimpeded until 2040;
In 2040, Man is granted habeas relief based on a Brady violation.
OTARMA's advocacy for the Arrest-Conviction Rule would result in an illogical conclusion in this over-simplified example. Insurer A, who supplied coverage for only one qualifying year (the

year of conviction), would be responsible for resulting damages. Insurer B, despite supplying coverage for 19 of the 20 policy years, would pay nothing. The example is not far-fetched. At minimum in this case, the OTARMA policies were in place for 17 years of Gillispie's 22-year incarceration (1996-2013).9
The Continuous Trigger Rule would take these factors into consideration and provide this Court with the flexibility to fashion a just remedy in accordance with sound public policy and the plain language of coverage documents. To accept OTARMA and National Casualty's position, on the other hand, will harm not only Miami Township but potentially Mr. Gillispie himself. The Continuous Trigger Rule is superior to the other isolated trigger options available to this Court and is best suited to deal with the complex nature of Brady violations and resulting insurance disputes. Therefore, this Court should apply the Continuous Trigger Rule in this case.
E. ALTERNATIVELY, IF THIS COURT DOES NOT APPLY THE CONTINUOUS TRIGGER RULE, THE EXONERATION RULE IS A MORE DESIRABLE ALTERNATIVE TO THE ARREST- CONVICTION RULE.
As is discussed supra, there are two other approaches to determining insurance coverage for civil rights claims like Mr. Gillispie's. Under the Arrest-Conviction Rule, Section 1983 claims and related state law claims trigger insurance coverage under policies in effect when the injury first occurs, i.e., the time of arrest or conviction. See e.g., City of Erie, Pa. v. Guaranty Nat. Ins. Co., 109 F.3d 156 (3d Cir. 1997). The Exoneration Rule, in contrast, finds that these civil rights claims trigger insurance policies in effect when the underlying proceeding is terminated in the plaintiff's favor, i.e., exoneration. See Am. Safety Cas. Ins. Co. v. City of Waukegan, 678 F.3d 475, 481 (7th Cir.2012) (applying Illinois law) ("One episode of malicious prosecution [or constitutional violations leading to wrongful conviction] has just one trigger: exoneration"); Nat'l
