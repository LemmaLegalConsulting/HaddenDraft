I. INTRODUCTION.
Despite the fact that the defendants, Julia Notarian and Charles Rossbach (jointly, "Defendants"), remain record the legal title holders of the property locate at 8127 Cedar Road, Chesterland, Ohio 44026 (the "Property"), the Estate of Anthony Notarian ("Plaintiff"), improperly seeks to evict them. Even more troubling is the fact that Plaintiff's alleged standing to evict is based on a non-final order from the Probate Court which is not subject to execution. Plaintiff's vindictive filing has the sole goal to effectuate a taking of the Property from Defendants to deprive them of their fundamental property rights and investment. Because there are genuine issues of material fact, Plaintiff's Motion for Partial Summary Judgment (the "Motion") should be denied.

III. LAW AND ARGUMENT.
A. Plaintiff Cannot Meet Its Burden Under Civil Rule 56(C) To Deprive Defendants Of Their Property Rights.
Rule 56(C) of the Ohio Rules of Civil Procedure provides that summary judgment may only be granted upon a trial court's determination that:
No genuine issue of material fact remains to be litigated; (2) The moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law; and (3) It appears from the evidence that reasonable minds could come to but one conclusion when viewing such evidence most strongly in favor of the party against whom the motion for summary judgment is made, that conclusion is adverse to that party.11
The standard for summary judgment requires that the court must construe the evidence most strongly in favor of the nonmoving party. Thus, the moving party may not make conclusory assertions that the nonmoving party has no evidence to prove its claim.12 The moving party must specifically point to some evidence of the type listed in Ohio Civil Rule 56(E) which demonstrates that the nonmoving party cannot support its claim. Upon satisfaction of this requirement, the nonmoving party must set forth specific facts showing that there is a genuine issue for trial.13
Based on the facts and the law, Plaintiff cannot meet the heavy burden of not only the granting of its affirmative claim but the denial of Defendants' right to present their defenses so that Plaintiff can effectuate what would be a judicial taking of their property.

B. The Court Cannot Grant Summary Judgment Based On A Non-Final Order Issued By The Probate Court.
Plaintiff argues that the Court should grant summary judgment based on the Probate Court's non-final order and rulings. For its Complaint, Plaintiff seeks to evict Defendants and also seeks money damages. These actions are in execution based on the Probate Court's non-final order and rulings. It is axiomatic that a trial court cannot execute on a judgment that is not final and appealable.14 In fact, "absent an express determination pursuant to Civ.R. 54(B), a judgment adjudicating fewer than all of the claims or parties does not terminate the action with respect to any party or claim, and that judgment is subject to revision throughout the remainder of the proceedings."15 Therefore, any such judgment is not subject to execution.16 Defendants' arguments in this regard are not novel as the case law relating to non-final orders is robust and long standing.17
Plaintiff attempts to bootstrap its argument by alleging that the deed executed by Defendants passes title to Plaintiff. This argument is disingenuous because the subject deed was ordered to be delivered and held by the Court. No control of said deed was given to Plaintiff. Furthermore, the deed was only signed when Defendants faced a contempt order based on the prior non-final order issued by the Probate Court. Therefore any argument in this regard are without merit.

IV. CONCLUSION.
In ruling on the Motion, this Court should be guided by the high standard under Civil Rule 56. The facts and law demonstrate that summary judgment is not appropriate as to the relief sought by Plaintiff based on a non-final order so as to deny Defendants' access to the Property and cause a taking. Accordingly, the Motion should be denied.
