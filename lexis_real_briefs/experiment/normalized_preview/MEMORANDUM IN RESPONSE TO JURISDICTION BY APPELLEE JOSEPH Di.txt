The Supreme Court of Ohio exercises its discretionary review only with regard to "cases of public or great general interest," or cases involving "a substantial constitutional question." Article IV, Section 2(B)(2) of the Ohio Constitution; S.Ct.Prac.R. 7.02(C)(2). Appeals presenting such serious and unresolved legal issues must be distinguished from those cases in which the controlling law is well established and the outcome is important only to the parties. Because this case falls squarely in the latter category, jurisdiction should be declined.

Appellant Joseph Licitri ("Licitri") denigrates the courts below and maligns Appellee Joseph DiBaggio ("DiBaggio") and his legal counsel in his Jurisdictional Brief. Licitri suggests that the courts below, counsel, and even the Ohio ( sic) Bar Association, through "sheer ignorance or legal incompetence" conspired to deprive him of his constitutional right to a trial by jury. But this case presents neither a constitutional question nor a matter of great public or general interest, and Licitri's rhetoric does not make it so. The courts below correctly applied the Rules of Civil Procedure and settled precedent in determining that Licitri's third-party legal malpractice claims failed as a matter of law under Civ.R. 56(C).

A. There is no issue of public or great interest presented here. Licitri's claims failed on their merits for lack of Civ.R. 56(E) evidence.

This case presents no question of public or great general interest because it is specific to the relatively unique circumstances presented herein and, therefore, of interest only to the parties involved; and because the Court of Appeals' rationale was based on settled precedent, rules of law, and the Rules of Civil Procedure. The court below correctly held that DiBaggio's motion for summary judgment was supported by an affidavit as required by Civ.R. 56(E) and that Licitri failed to provide any Civ.R. 56(E) evidence in response. Simply put, DiBaggio met his initial burden on summary judgment and Licitri did not. The underlying rationale is not a "made up procedural argument" as Licitri suggests at page 8 of his Memorandum in Support of Jurisdiction. There was no conspiracy to commit fraud or judicial bias against a pro se litigant. The courts below applied settled law to the facts presented on summary judgment and properly dismissed Licitri's third-party malpractice claims pursuant to Civ.R. 56(C) and (E).

This case presents an example of the judiciary functioning properly in the face of Licitri's continual personal ad hominem attacks on DiBaggio, undersigned counsel, and the courts below, and does not present an issue of public or great general interest that this Court can redress in this proffered appeal. Rather, the courts below acted properly and the case was correctly resolved pursuant to Civ.R. 56(C).

B. This case does not involve a substantial constitutional question.

The right to a jury trial is only enforceable where there are factual issues to be tried. A trial court does not violate the Ohio Constitution by granting summary judgment when no material issues of fact exist for a jury to decide. It is well-established that "[s]ummary judgment pursuant to Civ.R. 56 is another method available to a party seeking to avoid a trial and is used when the facts of a case are allegedly undisputed." Parrish v. Jones, 2013- Ohio-5224, P 13; Huntington Natl. Bank v. Thompson, 2014-Ohio-5168, P 20 (2d. Dist.); Tschantz v. Ferguson, 97 Ohio App.3d 693, 713 (8th Dist. 1994); Conley v. Willis, 2001- Ohio-2410 (4th Dist.). Accordingly, the proper granting of a motion for summary judgment does not abridge an individual's constitutional right to a jury trial. Western Credit Union v. Johnson, 2002-Ohio-4991 (4th Dist.).

No plaintiff, including Licitri, has an absolute constitutional right to a jury trial with respect to every claim or cause of action asserted in a complaint. Licitri failed to meet his reciprocal burden on summary judgment, thus there were no factual issues to be tried as a matter of law. Licitri proposes that a trial by jury must always be permitted in cases involving an officer of the court (i.e. attorney) and a pro se litigant. ( Memo. in Support of Jurisdiction, pg. 8). But this is not the law, nor is this proposition supported by logic, reason, or precedent. The courts below acted properly; the case was settled on its merits because Licitri failed to present any Civ.R. 56(E) evidence in response to DiBaggio's motion for summary judgment. There is nothing here for the Court to review.

II.  STATEMENT OF FACTS

This lawsuit arose out of a dispute between Licitri, a former homeowner in a condominium association, Independence Place South Condominium Association ("IPSCA" or "Association"), and the Association's legal counsel, Defendant-Appellee Joseph DiBaggio, Esq., arising from DiBaggio's representation of the Association in legal matters.

A dispute arose between Licitri and the Association regarding whether Licitri could operate his business, Licitri Piano Studio, from his unit. The Association's management company sent Licitri a letter asking him to cease operating his piano business from his unit. Months later, in October 2021, Attorney DiBaggio, on behalf of the Association, sent a Notice to Cure demanding that Licitri cease and desist operating his piano business from his unit in violation of the Association's Declaration and Bylaws. Licitri thereafter sent a series of letters to the Association via Attorney DiBaggio claiming that the Association failed to follow proper procedures relative to the Notice to Cure.

Licitri sued Attorney DiBaggio and others alleging that by sending the October 2021 Notice to Cure, Attorney DiBaggio violated various laws and provisions of the Code of Professional Responsibility and the Ohio Condominium Act. Licitri's Amended Complaint asserted claims for gross negligence and intentional infliction of emotional distress ("IIED").

Licitri did not conduct any discovery or depose any party or non-party witnesses. After fact discovery concluded, DiBaggio moved for summary judgment. In dismissing Licitri's claims the trial court correctly observed that a party moving for summary judgment bears the initial burden of pointing to the parts of the record that show the absence of a genuine issue of material fact. The trial court noted that DiBaggio's motion was supported by DiBaggio's own affidavit, the Association's Declaration and Bylaws, Licitri's verified interrogatory responses, and correspondence attached to Licitri's Complaint. In contrast, Licitri failed to provide any Civ.R. 56(E) evidence in opposition to DiBaggio's motion. While DiBaggio offered testimony via a sworn affidavit that he never represented Licitri and acted at all times in accordance with the standard of care, Licitri failed to provide any Civ.R. 56(E) evidence in response. For this reason alone, the trial court properly granted summary judgment to DiBaggio.

The trial court separately found that Licitri's IIED claim failed because "the act of sending a letter [is not] 'outrageous' but merely common practice to place an individual on notice of a violation." The court also dismissed Licitri's gross negligence claim because it stemmed from DiBaggio's legal representation and attorneys are immune from third- party claims arising from the good faith provision of legal services to a client.

Licitri appealed to the Ninth District Court of Appeals. In a well-reasoned opinion, the appellate court affirmed, holding that Licitri failed to offer Civ.R. 56 evidence in response to DiBaggio's motion for summary judgment, lacked standing to assert a third- party claim against an opponent's legal counsel, and admitted that he did not seek treatment for emotional distress.

III. RESPONSE TO LICITRI'S PROPOSITIONS OF LAW:

A. Response to Proposition of Law No. 1: Third-parties to the attorney-client relationship not in privity with the client lack standing to assert legal malpractice claims, and bare, unsubstantiated assertions of fraud, bad faith, collusion, or malice, as claimed by Licitri herein, cannot be used to circumvent the privity requirement.

Third parties to the attorney-client relationship (such as Licitri) cannot state a claim against an attorney for conduct related to the legal representation of a client. It is well-established that "[a]ttorneys in Ohio enjoy a qualified immunity from liability to a third party arising out of acts he or she takes while representing a client." Ryan v. Wright, 2007-Ohio-942, P 8 (10th Dist.), quoting Scholler v. Scholler, 10 Ohio St.3d 98 (1984), paragraph one of the syllabus. This rule, known as the strict privity rule, ensures that attorneys may represent their clients without the threat of suit from third parties who may have contrary interests (like Licitri). Shoemaker v. Gindlesberger, 2008-Ohio-2012, P 14-15.

Here, Licitri's claims against DiBaggio fell within the type of third-party, non-client actions courts have repeatedly rejected for lack of standing. The courts below correctly determined that DiBaggio's "actions clearly stem from his legal representations to IPSCA." (Sept. 13, 2023 Order, T.d.91, 60). That holding is directly in line with established case law holding that all claims against an attorney relating to legal representation or advice are claims for legal malpractice. Licitri did not allege that he was ever represented by or had an attorney-client relationship with DiBaggio-he alleged the opposite. Ohio law does not permit a third party to drag an attorney into litigation merely because the non-client disapproves of the strategy pursued or the representation the lawyer provided to his client. As a matter of law, Licitri could not maintain any claim against DiBaggio for his legal representation of the Association and summary judgment was correctly granted in DiBaggio's favor.

Licitri could not circumvent Ohio's strict privity requirement via unsupported allegations of fraud and malice arising from DiBaggio's representation of his client on summary judgment. "The necessity for privity may be overridden if special circumstances such as 'fraud, bad faith, collusion or other malicious conduct' are present." Shoemaker, 2008-Ohio-2012, P 11 (citing Simon v. Zipperstein, 32 Ohio St.3d 74, 77 (1987)). However, a bare, unsubstantiated assertion of fraud as claimed by Licitri here cannot be used to circumvent the privity requirement. The record on summary judgment is completely devoid of any evidence that DiBaggio acted with malice or engaged in fraud sufficient to overcome Ohio's strict privity requirement.

In Omega Riggers, the court addressed the malice required in connection with a third-party legal malpractice claim:

In our opinion, malice, as a substitute for an attorney-client relationship, cannot be predicated on actions by the attorney that the attorney is permitted to take, or even negligently may take, as part of the representation of plaintiffs' adversarial client. To constitute malice, the actions of the attorney must include a disregard of rights that the attorney, not the client, is required to protect and must include harm beyond that which legal action necessarily may inflict.

In most circumstances, an attorney is not obligated to protect the rights of an adversary. Undoubtedly, every lawyer who throws a family out into the cold in the dead of winter by pursuing a forcible-entry-and-detainer action has a great probability of causing harm. That scenario does not result in malpractice liability. Therefore, in our view, to constitute malice as a conscious disregard for the rights of others causing substantial harm that will suffice to substitute for an attorney-client relationship, facts must exist that demonstrate extra-legal activity .

Omega Riggers & Erectors, Inc. v. Koverman, 2016-Ohio-2961 (2d Dist.) (emphasis added). Here, Licitri made no serious effort to demonstrate the kind of "malicious circumstances" that would allow the filing of a malpractice case against opposing counsel. Licitri did not allege that DiBaggio engaged in any "extra-legal activity" or did anything other than advance the interests of his client, the Association, relative to the enforcement of the Association's rules, regulations, and bylaws. Corresponding with an adversary like Licitri is not improper, and even if it was negligent, under Omega Riggers, claims entirely based on DiBaggio's role as the Association's (not Licitri's) legal counsel cannot stand, even under the malice exception. While Licitri may disagree with the legal strategy asserted by the Association, that is not the kind of "special circumstance" or malicious conduct necessary to override the necessity for privity as a matter of law. Thus, summary judgment was properly granted in DiBaggio's favor.

B. Response to Proposition of Law No. 2: Summary judgment is appropriate where the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, written admissions, affidavits, transcripts of evidence, and written stipulations of fact establish that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact.

Summary judgment is properly granted pursuant to Civ.R. 56(C) when a review of the pleadings, deposition testimony, and other evidentiary materials demonstrates that: (1) no genuine issue as to any material fact exists; (2) the party moving for summary judgment is entitled to judgment as a matter of law; and (3) viewing the evidence most strongly in favor of the non-moving party, reasonable minds can only reach one conclusion, a conclusion adverse to the non-moving party. Temple v. Wean United, Inc., 50 Ohio St.2d 317, 327 (1977).

While Licitri styled his claims as "gross negligence" and "IIED," all claims against an attorney arising from the provision of legal services must be construed as legal malpractice. Illinois Natl. Ins. Co. v. Wiles, Boyle, Burkholder & Bringardner Co., L.P.A., 2010-Ohio-5872, P 15 (10th Dist.); Sandor v. Marks, 2014-Ohio-685, P 10 (9th Dist.)

It is by now "well settled in Ohio that in order to prevail on a legal malpractice claim a plaintiff must demonstrate, through expert testimony , by a preponderance of the evidence, that the representation of the attorney failed to meet the prevailing standard of care, and that the failure proximately caused damage or loss to the client." McWilliams v. Schumacher, 2013-Ohio-29, P 22 (8th Dist.) (emphasis added); see also Nu-Trend Homes v. Law Offices of DeLibera, Lyons & Bibbo, 2003-Ohio-1633, P 35 (10th Dist.) ("Expert testimony is normally necessary to establish both that the attorney accused of malpractice has failed to conform with the standard required by law and that the attorney's conduct was the proximate cause of the damage or loss claimed as the basis for redress by the complaining party."). Without expert testimony, a jury cannot ascertain the standard of care required of the attorney or whether a breach of that standard caused damages. Goldberg v. Mittman, 2007-Ohio-6599, P 11 (10th Dist.) ("Expert testimony is required so that the trier of fact does not have to speculate on the standard of care, particularly in a complex case involving * * * [matters] which are normally not within the realm of understanding of the layman.").

Accordingly, "an affidavit from the acting attorney is a legally sufficient basis upon which to grant a motion for summary judgment in a legal malpractice action absent any opposing affidavit of a qualified expert witness for the plaintiff." Yates v. Barilla, 2012- Ohio-3876, P 8 (9th Dist.). This is because "a defendant in a legal malpractice action possesses the required skill and knowledge to testify as to whether he or she met the applicable standard of care applicable to the client and that an independent expert is not required to defend a malpractice action." Roselle v. Nims, 2003-Ohio-630, P 31 (10th Dist.). Ohio courts have therefore "uniformly held that summary judgment is warranted when an attorney-defendant presents his own or an independent expert's affidavit and the plaintiff fails to respond with an opposing expert affidavit." Dillon v. Siniff, 2012- Ohio-910, P 22 (4th Dist.) (providing numerous supporting citations).

The case of Toliver v. Duwel, 2012-Ohio-846 (2d Dist.), proves the point. In that case, the defendant-attorney filed for summary judgment, supporting the motion with his own affidavit averring he acted properly during the course of representation. The plaintiff failed to present contrary expert testimony. Id. at P 37. The trial court granted judgment in the attorney's favor and the appellate court affirmed, holding:

The trial court was also correct when it concluded that there are no genuine issues of material fact precluding summary judgment for [the attorney] on claims about the quality of his representation or his billing. These are not issues where a breach would be obvious, nor are they matters within the common knowledge of layperson. Because [plaintiff] failed to present any expert testimony to support her claims, summary judgment on [the attorney's] behalf was proper.

Id. at P 59.

Just as in Toliver, whether DiBaggio owed any duties to Licitri or breached the standard of care when representing the Association falls outside the knowledge of laymen. DiBaggio offered an expert opinion that he conformed to the standard of care and charged a proper and reasonable fee. Absent contrary expert testimony, the trial court properly found that Licitri failed to create an issue of fact for trial. (Sept. 13, 2023 Order, T.d.91, No. 60, pg. 7).

Here, Licitri's Amended Complaint alleged that DiBaggio provided grossly negligent representation to the Association and that Licitri was damaged as a result of DiBaggio's conduct. To create an issue of fact for trial, Licitri was required to present expert testimony that DiBaggio breached the applicable standard of care and proximately caused Licitri damage. DiBaggio averred that he exercised the skill, care, and ability ordinarily possessed and exercised by other similarly situated attorneys during the course of representing the Association, did not owe any duty to or breach any duty owed to Licitri, and did not proximately cause Licitri any damage. (DiBaggio Aff., Ex. A to DiBaggio's MSJ, T.d.91, No. 45). Licitri did not present contrary expert testimony, identify an expert, or provide a written expert report. Indeed, as noted by the courts below, Licitri entirely failed to present any Civ.R. 56(E) evidence at all. Licitri argues that he could have established a genuine issue of fact had he been able to cross examine DiBaggio. ( Mem. in Support of Jurisdiction, pg. 9). But Licitri in fact had that opportunity during the discovery period and chose not to depose DiBaggio or any other party or non-party. Licitri failed to rebut DiBaggio's testimony, and DiBaggio was properly granted summary judgment.

C. Response to Proposition of Law No. 3: A court does not exhibit bias toward a pro se litigant by applying the law to the facts presented on summary judgment.

"'[P]ro se litigants are presumed to have knowledge of the law and legal procedures and that they are held to the same standard as litigants who are represented by counsel.'" State ex rel. Neil v. French, 2018-Ohio-2692, P 10. If courts were to treat pro se litigants differently, the court would depart from its duty of impartiality and prejudice the handling of the case as it relates to other litigants represented by counsel. State v. Vaduva, 2016-Ohio-3362, P 42 (2d Dist.). But that is precisely what Licitri asks this Court to do: create a special rule of law exempting legal malpractice claims brought by pro se plaintiffs from the purview of Civ.R 56. Licitri is not entitled to an exemption from the Rules of Civil

Procedure based on his status as a pro se litigant. Nor did the lower courts engage in fraud, conspiracy, or violate Licitri's constitutional rights by applying settled law to the facts of this case. Licitri's suggestions of impropriety are entirely unfounded and do not support this Court's jurisdiction.

IV. CONCLUSION

This is not a case of public or great general interest nor does it involve a substantial constitutional question. Despite the Ninth District's correct application of well-settled precedent and rules, Licitri portrays its ruling as eradicating his inviolate constitutional right to a trial by jury. But Licitri presents no inter- or intra-district conflicts or inconsistently applied Ohio decisions for this Court's consideration. No clarification is needed on the issues presented in Licitri's Propositions of Law. Licitri's tin foil conspiracy theories are without merit and, for the foregoing reasons, jurisdiction should be declined.
