REGULAR CALENDAR

ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR

I. The trial court erred in failing to apply the unambiguous meaning of the phrase "not subject to judicial process" as used in R.C. 2307.78(B)(1). Stip. Rec. No. 102 at 28; Stip. Rec. No. 108.

STATEMENT OF ISSUES

1. May a trial court interpret an unambiguous statute? (Assignment of Error I)

2. Does the term "judicial process" have a meaning different from its plain meaning when used in R.C. 2307.78(B)(1)? (Assignment of Error I)

3. Is a petitioner in bankruptcy or a petitioner whose debts have been discharged subject to judicial process in courts in this state? (Assignment of Error I)

STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Under Ohio law, a supplier of a defective product, such as Appellee, may be held strictly liable "as if it were the manufacturer of that product" if the manufacturer "is not subject to judicial process in this state." Manufacturers whose products Appellee supplied are not subject to judicial process pursuant to the well-established meaning of that term. The trial court nonetheless dismissed Appellant's strict liability claims against Appellee, under an admittedly "strained" interpretation of the statute.

STATEMENT OF FACTS

Evelyn Potts filed suit in this case more than three years ago, seeking recovery for the injuries of her husband, Charles Potts, who died from mesothelioma in November 2002. 1 Over the course of his career, Mr. Potts had been exposed to asbestos-containing products manufactured by Johns-Manville Corporation, The Celotex Corporation as successor in interest to Philip Carey Manufacturing Company, and Owens-Corning Fiberglas Corporation. 2 Exposure to asbestos fibers from these manufacturers' products contributed to Mr. Potts's fatal disease. 3 Mrs. Potts could not and did not sue these manufacturers, however, since these companies had all filed for bankruptcy and, in the case of Johns-Manville and Celotex Corporation, had their debts statutorily discharged. 4

Mrs. Potts sued The Edward R. Hart Company, as the supplier of products manufactured by the bankrupt companies. 5 Mrs. Potts prevailed on Hart's Motion for Summary Judgment, and the parties prepared for trial, scheduled to begin on February 13, 2006. 6 Before trial, Hart moved the trial court to prevent application of the relevant statute, R.C. 2307.78(B), by which the legislature provided for a supplier to be held strictly liable "as if" it were the manufacturer of the products. 7 This provision holds a supplier vicariously liable for the actions of an absent manufacturer in eight enumerated situations. Mrs. Potts initially sought to hold Hart liable for the actions of the bankrupt manufacturers on two, independently sufficient grounds listed in the statute: that the absent manufacturers were "not subject to judicial process" in Ohio courts 8 and that she was "unable to enforce a judgment against the manufacturer[s] . . . due to actual or asserted insolvency of the manufacturer[s]." 9

Hart argued that the "actual or asserted insolvency" grounds in R.C. 2307.78(B)(2) did not apply to the bankrupt manufacturers and that the statute would be unconstitutionally retroactive as applied in this case. 10 Hart principally relied on the General Assembly's revision and recodification of the statute in 1987. 11 The prior version of the statute had explicitly held suppliers vicariously liable for the actions of manufacturers that had "filed for bankruptcy prior to the commencement of or during the pendency of the action." 12 Hart argued that the legislature's removal of this explicit reference to bankruptcy evinced a legislative intent to exclude bankrupt companies from the scope of the statutory provision. 13 In order to simplify the issues, Mrs. Potts agreed to proceed only under R.C. 2307.78(B)(1), the provision holding Hart liable because the bankrupt manufacturers were "not subject to judicial process" in Ohio, pursuant to the plain meaning of that term and the undisputed fact that the bankrupt manufacturers are not subject to judicial process in this state. 14

After extensive briefing on the issues, the trial court issued its oral ruling. The trial court first acknowledged that the absent manufacturers which had sought bankruptcy protection were "not subject to judicial process" in Ohio, since "any judicial process that is issued . . . would be void." 15 The court also noted that there was "not a single citation or reference" of legislative history instructive on the issue before it. 16 The court nevertheless granted Hart's motion, finding that the term "not subject to judicial process" did not include the bankrupt manufacturers, drawing on the court's interpretation of the legislative history and cases from foreign jurisdictions interpreting different statutory language. 17 The court concluded the hearing by admitting that its ruling was a "strained interpretation of the term judicial process." 18 The trial court subsequently issued its judgment entry, deeming Hart's Cross-Motion to Exclude Evidence of Vicarious Liability Under R.C. 2307.78(B)(1) a "Motion for Partial Summary Judgment" under Ohio Civil Rule 56, granting the motion, and, therefore, granting judgment to Hart on Mrs. Potts's strict liability claim against Hart. 19 The court ruled that Hart's motion regarding the statute's retroactivity was rendered moot by its ruling. 20

STANDARD OF REVIEW

An appellate court reviews a trial court's granting of a motion for summary judgment under a de novo standard of review. Am. Financial Serv. Assn. v. Cleveland (2004), 159 Ohio App.3d 489, 495, 824 N.E.2d 553. A trial court's interpretation of a statute is also subject to a de novo standard of review. Star Bank, N.A. v. Matthews (2001), 144 Ohio App.3d 246, 250, 759 N.E.2d 1274;Cleveland Hts. v. Jones (Feb. 2, 2006), Cuyahoga App. No. 86313, 2006 WL 256638 (slip copy).

I.  THE TRIAL COURT ERRED BY STRAINING TO INTERPRET AN UNAMBIGUOUS STATUTORY TERM.

The trial court's ruling, and therefore this entire appeal, revolves around the meaning of the phrase "judicial process" as used in R.C. 2307.78(B)(1). Since this phrase has a "clear and definite meaning," the trial court erred in considering legislative history and other extrinsic sources to divine a different meaning.

A.  If a Term is Unambiguous, a Trial Court Must Simply Apply It and Not Interpret It.

In applying a statute, the court's inquiry "begins with the statutory text, and ends there as well if the text is unambiguous. Thus, when a statute is unambiguous in its terms, courts must apply it rather than interpret it." State ex rel. Plain Dealer Publishing Co. v. Cleveland (2005), 106 Ohio St.3d 70, 76-77, 831 N.E.2d 987 (citations omitted). A court may only use "aids in construction" such as legislative history and "the circumstances under which the statute was enacted" when a statute is ambiguous. R.C. 1.49. The Ohio Supreme Court has explicitly rejected the idea that courts should engage in statutory interpretation of any kind when a term is unambiguous:

But the intent of the law-makers is to be sought first of all in the language employed, and if the words be free from ambiguity and doubt, and express plainly, clearly and distinctly, the sense of the law-making body, there is no occasion to resort to other means of interpretation. The question is not what did the general assembly intend to enact, but what is the meaning of that which it did enact. That body should be held to mean what it has plainly expressed, and hence no room is left for construction.

State v. Hairston (2004), 101 Ohio St.3d 308, 309-10, 804 N.E.2d 471 (internal citation omitted) (emphasis added).

B.  The Term "Judicial Process" is an Unambiguous Term, Referring to the Institution of Court Proceedings.

"Judicial process" is not defined in R.C. 2307.78 or in any other Ohio statute. When a term is left undefined, the term should be given its "common, everyday meaning." MP Star Financial, Inc. v. Cleveland State Univ. (2005), 107 Ohio St.3d 176, 178, 837 N.E.2d 758. "A statutory term susceptible of more than one definition should be afforded its plain and ordinary meaning." Performing Arts School of Metro. Toledo, Inc. v. Wilkins (2004), 104 Ohio St.3d 284, 286-87, 819 N.E.2d 649. Ohio courts use dictionary definitions to determine the "common, everyday meaning" of a term. See MP Star Financial, Inc., 107 Ohio St.3d at 178 (quoting from the American Heritage Dictionary and Black's Law Dictionary); State v. Tuomala (2004), 104 Ohio St.3d 93, 96, 818 N.E.2d 272, 276 (quoting from Black's Law Dictionary); State ex rel. Pontillo v. Pub. Emp. Retirement Sys. Bd. (2003), 98 Ohio St.3d 500, 505, 787 N.E.2d 643 (quoting Webster's Third New International Dictionary and Black's Law Dictionary); State v. Cravens (1988), 42 Ohio App.3d 69, 72, 536 N.E.2d 686 (quoting from Ballentine's Law Dictionary).

The Ohio Supreme Court's recent decision in MP Star Financial is instructive on the proper way to apply an undefined statute. In that case, the Court considered whether certain monetary payments were "transfers" within the meaning of R.C. 1309.109(D)(14). The court began by observing that the statute did not define "transfer" and that undefined terms should be given their common meaning. The court turned to the American Heritage Dictionary and Black's Law Dictionary to ascertain the common meaning of the word. 107 Ohio St.3d at 178. Citing the official comments to a former version of the statute, MP Star argued that the word "transfer" did not include payments of money. The court rejected this argument, explaining that "because the meaning of R.C. 1309.109(D)(14) is clear and unambiguous, we will not attempt to ascertain a meaning different from that on the face of the statute by examining the comments." Id. at 179.

The term "judicial process" is likewise unambiguous. Appellant provided the court with two dictionary definitions of the term. 21 The American Heritage Dictionary, one of the objective sources consulted by the court in MP Star Financial, defines "process" in the legal context as a "summons or writ ordering a defendant to appear in court." AMERICAN HERITAGE DICTIONARY (2 Ed. 1985) 987. Black's Law Dictionary, another source cited by the MP Star Financial court, defines "judicial process" as "the writ, summons, mandate, or other process which is used to inform the defendant of the institution of proceedings against him and to compel his appearance, in either civil or criminal cases." BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (6 Ed. 1990) 1205. These definitions are consistent with the use of the term in other Ohio statutes, in which "judicial process" is universally used to refer to the institution of court proceedings. See R.C. 303.51; R.C. 1307.39; R.C. 1309.602; R.C. 1309.609; R.C. 1310.31; R.C. 1310.71; R.C. 1333.34; R.C. 1703.01; R.C. 1923.02; R. C. 3752.113; R.C. 3903.01; R.C. 4301.29; R.C. 4981.15; R.C. 5149.21.

Despite these unanimous indications that "judicial process" should be afforded its clear meaning, Appellee argued at trial that the term is a "strange word" and should instead be defined as "personal jurisdiction." 22 All the sources Appellee cited in support of its argument, however, referred to lack of personal jurisdiction as just one example of a situation in which a defendant would not be subject to judicial process. 23 One source noted that the supplier statute would apply "[i]f, for example, the manufacturer of a product is unavailable in Ohio due to lack of personal jurisdiction." 24 Another case made clear that personal jurisdiction will only be an issue in select cases involving judicial process:

Therefore, the ultimate question is whether the manufacturer in the matter subjudice, Fujitsu, is subject to judicial process in this state. As we read this section and for purposes of this appeal only, 'judicial process' refers to personal jurisdiction and whether the trial court herein had personal jurisdiction over Fujitsu.

State Farm Fire & Cas. Co. v. Kupanoff Imports, Inc. (1992), 83 Ohio App.3d 278, 281, 614 N.E.2d 1072 (emphasis added). These sources simply do not support the argument that "judicial process" refers only to "personal jurisdiction."

C.  As a Matter of Law, the Bankrupt Manufacturers Are Not Subject to Judicial Process in this State.

Federal statutes state unequivocally that debtors who have filed bankruptcy petitions or received discharges in bankruptcy are not subject to judicial process. The bankruptcy petition operates as a stay of "the commencement or continuation, including the issuance or employment of process, of a judicial, administrative, or other action or proceeding against the debtor that was or could have been commenced before" the bankruptcy proceeding. Section 362(a)(1), Title 11, U.S.Code (emphasis added). The stay is in effect until the bankruptcy case is closed or dismissed or at the time a discharge is granted or denied, whichever comes earliest. Section 362(c)(2), Title 11, U.S.Code. This prohibition against service of process prevents a petitioner from being joined as a party to a suit. See Orix Credit Alliance, Inc. v. First Florida Bank, N.A. (M.D. Fla. 1992), 147 B.R. 594, 595 (petitioner "is not subject to service of process because the automatic stay of 11 U.S.C. § 362 currently bars [the petitioner] from being joined as a party").

Once a petitioner's case has been discharged, the discharge "operates as an injunction against the commencement or continuation of an action, the employment of process, or an act, to collect, recover or offset any such debt as a personal liability of the debtor, whether or not discharge of such debt is waived." Section 524(a)(2), Title 11, U.S.Code (emphasis added). "The injunction is to give complete effect to the discharge and to eliminate any doubt concerning the effect of the discharge as a total prohibition on debt collection efforts." Id., Revision Notes and Legislative Reports.

None of the manufacturers whose products Hart supplied-Johns-Manville Corporation, Celotox Corporation, and Owens Corning Fiberglas Corporation-are subject to judicial process in Ohio courts. Johns-Manville Corporation filed its petition in bankruptcy on August 26, 1982, 25 and had its debts statutorily discharged and its bankruptcy plan approved on December 22, 1986. 26 Celotox Corporation filed its voluntary petition in bankruptcy on October 12, 1990 27 and also had its debts discharged. Owens-Corning Fiberglas Corporation filed its voluntary petition in bankruptcy, which is still pending, on October 5, 2000. 28 According to federal bankruptcy law, none of these manufacturers are subject to judicial process.

As the trial court acknowledged, the manufacturers which had sought bankruptcy protection were "not subject to judicial process" in Ohio, since "any judicial process that is issued . . . would be void." 29 See Easley v. Pettibone Michigan Corp. (C.A.6 1993), 990 F.2d 905, 911; Curtis v. Payton (Feb. 5, 1999) Greene App. No. 98-CA-49, 1999 WL 57763 ("Generally, actions taken in violation of the automatic stay are void and without force and effect."); In re Schneiderman (Bkrtcy. D. Dist. Colo. 2000), 251 B.R. 740, 768 ("The debtor's discharge voided Chase's judgment [citation] and enjoins continuation of its employment of process to collect the debt [citation].").

Appellee did argue that Appellant should be required to request relief from the automatic stay with respect to Owens Corning Fiberglas, but this argument is incorrect as to Owens Corning and irrelevant to the other manufacturers. Appellant explained to the trial court that the judge who oversees that bankruptcy had previously denied a request to lift the automatic stay in similar circumstances. 30 A party is not required to take action that would be futile. State ex rel. Toledo v. Lucas Cty. Bd. of Elections (2002), 95 Ohio St.3d 73, 74-75, 765 N.E.2d 854. Moreover, the argument is not even theoretically applicable to Johns Manville or Celotex, whose debts have long since been discharged. No procedure exists to subject these companies to judicial process with respect to liabilities that were discharged in bankruptcy.

D.  The Trial Court Should Have Applied the Unambiguous Terms of the Statute to the Undisputed Facts of this Case.

As discussed above, the word "process" is used in both R.C. 2307.78 and the Bankruptcy Code to refer to the institution of court proceedings. Upon recognizing that court proceedings could not be instituted against the bankrupt manufacturers in this state, the trial court should have applied the statute as written. Instead, the trial court proceeded "to look at what the legislature has done to try to determine what the Ohio legislature meant by the utilization of that term." 31 The trial court expressly acknowledged how far it had to go to reach this conclusion:

A week ago if you told me about this decision, I would have said, boy, that is a strained interpretation of the term judicial process, but that's exactly what it is. When we strain as hard as we all have strained the last few days to really analyze the language employed, the history of the statute, I am constrained to find the way I do. 32

The court erred in "straining" at all. In the absence of any genuine ambiguity, the trial court should not have resorted to extrinsic sources. Inquiry "into the legislative intent, legislative history, public policy, the consequences of an interpretation, or any of the other factors identified in R.C. 1.49 is inappropriate absent an initial finding that the language of the statute is, itself, capable of more than one meaning. Thus, a court's understanding of legislative intent alone cannot make an otherwise clear statute ambiguous." In re Adoption of Baby Boy Brooks (2000), 136 Ohio App.3d 824, 829, 737 N.E.2d 1062 (citations omitted).

Even if the trial court had the authority to engage in statutory interpretation of an unambiguous term, the trial court failed to find any support in the legislative history for its ruling that the bankrupt manufacturers were "subject to judicial process" under R.C. 2307.78(B)(1). Instead, the trial court relied on the presumed intentions of lobbyists, reasoning that the original supplier statute "was in all probability a reaction to pleas from the Chamber of Commerce side of the lobby rather than the Plaintiff's lobby . . . ." 33 No Ohio court has ever suggested that the policy preferences of lobby groups should sway judicial interpretation of the laws.

The trial court also erred in considering cases interpreting terms that do not appear in the Ohio statute. The trial court relied on opinions by Judge McAnulty in Kentucky and Judge Freedman in New York, interpreting different statutes. 34 In both Parker v. Henry A. Petter Supply Co. (Ky. 2005), 165 S.W.3d 474, and Tancredi v. A.C. & S., Inc. (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 2002), 750 N.Y.S.2d 469, 194 Misc.2d 214, the statutes at issue used the term "jurisdiction," while the Ohio statute uses "judicial process." Compare N.Y. C.P.L.R. 1601 (plaintiff is required to prove "that with due diligence he or she will be unable to obtain jurisdiction over the absent party") and K.Y. REV. STAT. 411.340 (statute's applicability turns on whether "the manufacturer is identified and subject to the jurisdiction of the court") with R.C. 2307.78(B)(1) (holding supplier liable when a manufacturer is "not subject to judicial process in this state"). Both cases focused on the specific meaning of "jurisdiction." See Tancredi, 750 N.Y.S.2d at 479; Parker, 165 S.W.3d at 477.

Even assuming that Parker and Tancredi were correct interpretations of those statutes, the key term "jurisdiction" is not used in the Ohio statute. This legislative choice to use "judicial process" rather than "personal jurisdiction" can only be interpreted as intentional, particularly given the fact that the General Assembly has affirmatively chosen to use the latter term in more than a dozen other statutes. See R.C. 1335.11(E); 91; R.C. 3111.06; R.C. 3115.03; R.C. 3115.04; R.C. 3115.12; R.C. 3115.25; R.C. 3115.44(A)(1); R.C. 3115.48(A)(2); R.C. 3127.08; R.C. 3127.15(C); R.C. 3901.17(B); R.C. 3901.323(A); R.C. 3903.02(D).

The trial court relied heavily on its inferences regarding the legislature's deletion of the word "bankruptcy" from R.C. 2307.78(B) when the statute was revised in 1987. Even if the trial court had the authority to engage in such statutory interpretation given the clear and plain meaning of "judicial process," the court's conclusion is nevertheless unsupported by the legislative history. In fact, the court acknowledged that there was no instructive legislative history on the revision of the statute. 35

The word "bankruptcy" was deleted from the statute for a very obvious reason. In order to hold a supplier liable under the former version of the statute, a plaintiff had to show both that the manufacturer was not "subject to judicial process" and that the manufacturer had "filed for bankruptcy prior to the commencement of or during the pendency of the action [or had] been judicially declared insolvent due to an inability to pay its debts as they became due in the ordinary course of business." R.C. 2305.33(B)(2)(b) (repealed 1988). Since a bankrupt manufacturer would therefore be covered by the plain language of the revised 2307.78(B)(1), any specific reference to bankruptcy would have been superfluous.

By refusing to apply the unambiguous terms of the statute, the trial court effectively rejected "[t]he legislative judgment expressed by R.C. 2307.78(B) . . . that, in the eight circumstances listed, it is appropriate to make a product supplier 'vicariously' subject to the liability of the manufacturer of the product in question." 36 This legislative judgment was based on the theory that "although suppliers are given some protection from products liability under the statute, . . . such protection would not be allowed to deprive a claimant of any relief whatsoever." 37

II.  ANY ALTERNATIVE GROUNDS FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT NOT RULED UPON BY THE TRIAL COURT SHOULD NOT BE CONSIDERED.

A.  The Trial Court Did Not Consider Any Alternative Grounds for Summary Judgment.

In addition to its argument that the legislature meant "personal jurisdiction" when it used the term "judicial process" in R.C. 2307.78(B)(1), Appellee also argued that the statute would be unconstitutionally retroactive as applied in this case. The trial court did not rule on the issue, finding that Appellee's motion raising the retroactivity argument was rendered moot by the court's granting of Appellee's motion on the "judicial process" argument. 38 Because the trial court declined to rule on the retroactivity issue, this court should not reach it.

The court in Orvets v. Natl. City Bank, Northeast (1999), 131 Ohio App.3d 180, 722 N.E.2d 114, faced this exact situation. In Orvets, the appellee argued that "even if he was not entitled to summary judgment for the reasons the trial court granted him summary judgment, he was entitled to summary judgment on alternative grounds." Id. at 192-93. The trial court, however, had not ruled on these alternative grounds. The appellate court held that "it would be inappropriate for this court to consider the evidence presented by [appellees] and pass upon those grounds without the trial court first doing so." Orvets, 131 Ohio App.3d at 194. In the same way, it would be inappropriate for this Court to consider the retroactivity arguments advanced by Appellee, which the trial court held were "rendered moot" by its granting of Appellee's motion on other grounds.

B.  R.C. 2307.78(B)(1) is Not Retroactive.

Even if this Court elects to consider the retroactivity arguments advanced by Appellee, the arguments have no sound legal basis. Appellee essentially argued that because its sale of asbestos-containing products occurred prior to the effective date of the statute, the statute could not be applied in this case. 39 Since asbestos-related diseases have long latency periods, such an argument would effectively preclude the legislature from addressing asbestos cases at all. Appellee has offered no authority for this contention.

Ohio law "establishes a presumption that statutes are prospective in operation" and that "a statute may constitutionally be applied retrospectively . . . only after a threshold finding that the General Assembly expressly intended the statute to apply retrospectively." Bielat v. Bielat (2000), 87 Ohio St.3d 350, 353, 721 N.E.2d 28. But that presumption need not have been rebutted in this case because R.C. 2307.78 applies prospectively to impose strict liability on Appellee as a supplier.

The General Assembly made clear that R.C. 2307.78 should only be applied prospectively when it provided that the statute "shall apply only to product liability actions that are commenced on and after the effective date of this act and that are based on claims for relief that arise on or after the date." 1987 H 1, § 3, eff. 1-5-88 (emphasis added). This language "unequivocally express[es] the legislative intent that [the statute] is to operate prospectively, not retroactively." Scalf v. Hann (2004), Butler App. No. CA2004-02-040, 2004 WL 2029933, *2. Cf. Johnson v. Adams (1985), 18 Ohio St.3d 48, 49-50, 479 N.E.2d 866 (finding legislative intent for retroactivity in a statute which applied "irrespective of whether a child is born prior to, or on or after, the effective date of this act"); State ex rel. Kilbane v. Indus. Comm. of Ohio (2001), 91 Ohio St.3d 258, 259, 744 N.E.2d 708 (finding the legislative intent for retroactivity "obvious" in statute made applicable to all claims pending on the act's effective date).

The statute is also prospective as applied in this case. Under Ohio law, a claim for asbestos-related injuries does not "arise" until "the date on which the plaintiff is informed by competent medical authority that the plaintiff has been injured by such exposure, or upon the date on which, by the exercise of reasonable diligence, the plaintiff should have become aware that the plaintiff had been injured by the exposure, whichever date occurs first." See R.C. 2305.10 (eff. July 6, 2001). In this case, Appellant's claims did not arise until she received the results of the autopsy taken after Mr. Potts's death in November 2002, long after the effective date of the statute.

Appellee nonetheless complained at trial that R.C. 2307.78(B) would be retroactive as applied since the actions that form the basis of its liability-supplying asbestos-containing products manufactured by now-bankrupt companies-occurred before the effective date of the statute. Given the latency period in this and other asbestos cases, liability for actions that accrued only recently will often depend on events that occurred years, sometimes decades, ago. But "[a] statute is not retroactive merely because it draws upon antecedent facts for its operation." Lewis v. Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Maryland (1934), 292 U.S. 559, 571, 54 S. Ct. 848.

Ohio courts have sensibly found that statutory amendments that apply to cases that arise or are filed on or after the effective date of the act are prospective, regardless of whether those cases are based on events that occurred long ago. In this way, R.C. 2307.78 is not different from those statutes and is not retroactive in its language or its application. The plain meaning of R.C. 2307.78 shows that it is prospective both on its face and as applied in this case.

C.  R.C. 2307.78(B)(1) Does Not Violate the Ohio Constitution.

Even if this Court were to find that the General Assembly intended R.C. 2307.78 to apply retroactively, the retroactive application of the statute would not be unconstitutional because the statute only makes remedial changes in the allocation of liability for asbestos-related injuries. In Bielat v. Bielat (2000), 87 Ohio St.3d 350, 353, 721 N.E.2d 28, the Ohio Supreme Court emphasized that "retroactivity itself is not always forbidden by Ohio law. . . . Ohio courts have long recognized that there is a crucial distinction between statutes that merely apply retroactively (or 'retrospectively') and those that do so in a manner that offends our Constitution." Ohio courts have long held that remedial statutes "which merely substitute a new or more appropriate remedy for the enforcement of an existing right" may be applied retroactively. Van Fossen v. Babcock & Wilcox Co. (1988), 36 Ohio St.3d 100, 107, 522 N.E.2d 489, superseded on other grounds by R.C. 2745.01. See also In re Good Samaritan Hosp. (1995), 107 Ohio App.3d 351, 355-56, 668 N.E.2d 974 ("laws which merely substitute a new remedy for the enforcement of an existing right are 'remedial' in nature").

There is no question that, at the time Mr. Potts was exposed to asbestos-containing products supplied and installed by Appellee, the law recognized a right of action against suppliers of defective products. In 1965, the court noted the "general agreement" concerning a supplier's liability for harm caused by its product: "It is now generally agreed that a seller, or other supplier of chattels for a consideration, may be liable for harm to the person or property of a third person who may be expected to be in the vicinity of the chattel's probable use, if he has failed to exercise reasonable care to make the chattel safe for the use for which it is supplied." Lonzrick v. Republic Steel Corp. (1965), 1 Ohio App.2d 374, 376, 205 N.E.2d 92. And in Hargis v. Doe (1981), 3 Ohio App.3d 36, 37, 443 N.E.2d 1008, again before passage of R.C. 2307.78, the court acknowledged that a supplier was liable, like a manufacturer or seller, "for the damages proximately caused by the use of his product, in the manner and for the purpose for which it was supplied, if he fails to exercise reasonable care to give the user information which he has and which he should realize would be necessary to make the use of the product safe."

Suppliers like Appellee were also subject to strict liability before the enactment of R.C. 2307.78. In 1977, the Ohio Supreme Court expressly adopted Section 402A of the Restatement of the Law 2d, Torts (1965). See Temple v. Wean United, Inc. (1977), 50 Ohio St.2d 317, 322, 364 N.E.2d 267, superseded on other grounds by R.C. 2315.19. In doing so, the court recognized the longstanding legal principle that "[u]nder certain conditions a seller of a defective product who is not the manufacturer may be held strictly liable in tort for injuries caused by the product." Bakonyi v. Ralston Purina Co. (1985), 17 Ohio St.3d 154, 155, 478 N.E.2d 241. Liability may be imposed "although the seller has exercised all possible care in the preparation and sale of his product." Id. at 155-56. The rationale for imposing liability was that "[t]he retailer is in a more favorable position to bear the costs of accidents due to the defectively dangerous products he sells" and that "[i]f the manufacturer is insolvent or is a corporation that has been dissolved, or if the defect is one that is not traceable to the manufacturer, the loss is one that can best be borne by the retailer as a cost of doing business." Id. at 155, n. 1 (quoting Prosser & Keeton, Law of Torts (5 Ed. 1984) 706-07 Section 100).

A similar situation was addressed in French v. Dwiggins (1984), 9 Ohio St.3d 32,458 N.E.2d 827, which involved an amendment to the wrongful death statute to authorize the recovery of non-pecuniary damages. The Ohio Supreme Court rejected the contention that the statute created new causes of action and held that "the expansion of allowable damages in wrongful death actions . . . merely seeks to justly compensate those persons injured as a direct consequence of a wrongful death." Id. at 36. The court also noted that, because the defendant was already liable to the plaintiff, the defendant "could not reasonably be expected to conduct his affairs differently depending on which version of [the statute] was in effect." Id. at 35. Consequently, the statute's provisions were remedial and could be applied retroactively. R.C. 2307.78(B), which seeks to ensure full compensation of those injured by defective products, is likewise remedial. Even if the statute were deemed to be retroactive, such application would not violate the Ohio Constitution.

III. CONCLUSION

The trial court erred in granting summary judgment in favor of Appellee on Appellant's strict liability claim. Based on the unambiguous meaning of "judicial process," Appellant may hold Appellee liable as if it were the bankrupt manufacturers who are not subject to judicial process in courts in this state. Appellant respectfully urges the Court to reverse the trial court's judgment and remand the case for trial.
