MEMORANDUM
I. Statement of Relevant Facts and Procedural Posture
This case seeks the Court's assistance in determining whether CIC owes coverage (defense or indemnity) to Defendant Miami-Luken under a commercial general liability ("CGL") insurance contract between the two parties. CIC is already defending Miami-Luken under a Directors and Officers ("D and O") policy and intends to do so throughout the course of the underlying tort litigation which spurred the present declaratory judgment action. Therefore, Miami-Luken will be defended through trial or settlement of the underlying action regardless of the Court's decision here. Still, the parties need a resolution of whether or not this particular CGL Contract (the "Contract") provides coverage.
The underlying tort litigation was filed by the Attorney General for the State of West Virginia (the "AG" or "West Virginia AG") on June 26, 2012. This litigation was initially filed by Attorney General Darrell V. McGraw, Jr. Mr. McGraw, however, lost his re-election bid but the litigation is continuing to be prosecuted by current Attorney

General, Patrick Morrisey, who filed an Amended Complaint on January 2, 2014. A copy of this Amended Complaint is attached hereto for the Court's reference. We understand that a Second Amended Complaint was filed under seal in the West Virginia action and we are attempting to obtain a copy of that Second Amended Complaint but so far have not been able to do so. If we are able to do so, we will file a copy with the Court. We understand, however, from attorneys involved in that litigation that the Second Amended Complaint is substantively and materially the same as the First Amended Complaint which is attached hereto for the Court's reference.
In the West Virginia action, the West Virginia Attorney General is seeking recovery from a number of pharmaceutical manufacturers and distributors for what it deems an "epidemic" of prescription drug abuse in the state of West Virginia.1 The state blames these pharmaceutical manufacturers and distributors for certain economic damages it believes it has incurred due to this epidemic and lists five causes of action in its Amended Complaint, as follows:
Count 1: For injunctive relief for violations of responsibilities and duties under the West Virginia Uniform Controlled Substances Act;
Count 2: For damages resulting from negligence and violations of the West Virginia Uniform Controlled Substances Act;
Count 3: For violation of the West Virginia Consumer Credit and Protection Act and unfair methods of competition or unfair or deceptive acts or practices;
Count 4:
For public nuisance; and

Count 5: For Negligence.2

A grant of Summary Judgment is appropriate if: (1) No genuine issue of material fact remains to be litigated; (2) The moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law; and (3) The evidence demonstrates that reasonable minds can come to but one conclusion, and that conclusion is adverse to the party opposing the Motion.5 The court must view all the evidence, and the reasonable inferences to be drawn therein, in a light most favorable to the non-moving party.6 The moving party bears the initial burden of demonstrating that there are no genuine issues of material fact concerning an essential element of the opponent's case." If the moving party satisfies this burden, the non- moving party "must set forth specific facts showing that there is a genuine issue for trial."8
The parties agree that Ohio law applies to this Ohio Contract, entered into by two Ohio companies in Ohio, so no discussion of choice-of-law issues will be briefed here.

The insuring agreement of the CIC CGL policy requires that before Miami-Luken in entitled to coverage, it has to be "legally obligated to pay" damages.

This same argument was made by gun manufacturers in The City of Chicago v. Beretta USA Corporation, 213 III. 2d 351, 821 N.E. 2d 1099 (III. 2005) and the Illinois Supreme Court upheld summary judgment on behalf of the gun manufacturers and distributors.

In its decision, the Illinois Supreme Court held that gun manufacturers and distributors were not liable for the City of Chicago's economic losses for two reasons: (1) Because the manufacturers and distributors owed no legal duty to prevent the misuse of their products if the products were manufactured according to specifications and operated without mechanical defect; and (2) Because the court did not want to be responsible for an overtly exaggerated expansion of the public nuisance doctrine.
As to the duty factor, the court stated as follows:
Applying the four traditional [Negligence] factors [and] in light of public policy, we find no duty owed to the public at large, at least with respect to the manufacturer and distributor defendants. It is reasonably foreseeable, in a nation that permits private ownership of firearms, that criminals will obtain guns and it is not only likely, but inevitable, that injuries and death will result. It is less foreseeable to these defendants that the criminal conduct of individuals who illegally take firearms into a particular community will result in the creation of a public nuisance there. Further, despite Plaintiffs' suggestion that the only burden they would place on defendants is the loss of sales to criminals, the magnitude of the burden that plaintiffs seek to impose on the manufacturer and distributor defendants by altering their business practices is immense. Finally, plaintiffs predict only positive consequences if this duty is recognized-the City will be safer and lives will be saved. Such positive consequences are speculative at best, being based on the assumption that criminals will not be able to obtain guns manufactured by other companies and sold by other dealers. The negative consequences of judicially imposing a duty upon commercial enterprises to guard against the criminal misuse of their products by others will be an unprecedented expansion of the law of public nuisance. [Emphasis added -- citation omitted]
We hold, therefore, that with respect to the defendant manufacturers and distributors, plaintiffs have failed to state a cause of action for public nuisance predicated on negligence, because these defendants owe no duty to the City of Chicago or its residents to prevent their firearms from

"ending up in the hands of persons who use and possess them illegally." 821 N.E. 2d at 1126.
