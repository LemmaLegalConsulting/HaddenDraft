STATEMENT OF FACTS

In October of 2010, Ms. Masten resided at Harbor Crest Apartments, located at 24455 Lakeshore Boulevard, Euclid, Ohio, 44123 ("the premises"), managed by K&D Management LLC. (Def.'s Answer & Counterclaim at P 1). When Plaintiff and Defendant executed the lease agreement, Plaintiff informed Defendant that she is a victim of domestic violence, and as a result, she currently suffers from mental disabilities including Post Traumatic Stress Disorder, Anxiety, depression and physical disabilities including injuries to her foot and hand. (Def.'s Answer & Counterclaim at P 41).

Upon arriving on the premises, Ms. Masten witnessed another disabled resident, Marie Ann Thompson ("Ms. Thompson"), being discriminated against by the Defendant in regard to those disabilities. (Def.'s Answer & Counterclaim at P 10). Ms. Masten learned from the Defendant's employees that Ms. Thompson was not wanted on the property because of her disabilities, and that the Defendant essentially planned to evict her. (Def.'s Answer & Counterclaim at P 10). Ms. Masten began assisting Ms. Thompson in drafting and filing a charge with the Ohio Civil Rights Commission ("OCRC") against the Defendant for violations of Ohio Revised Code § 4112.02(H)(12) and 42 U.S.C. § 3617. (Def.'s Answer & Counterclaim at P 10). After assisting Ms. Thompson with her OCRC filing, and after K&D Managers learned of Ms. Masten assisting Ms. Thompsan with the OCRC charge, the Defendant began discriminating against Ms. Masten in various ways. (Def.'s Answer & Counterclaim at P 13).

In November of 2010, Ms. Masten filed her own charge with the Ohio Civil Rights Commission and the discriminatory actions ensued. (Def.'s Answer & Counterclaim at P 14). In February of 2011, the Defendant inquired whether Ms. Masten would consider dropping her own charge with the Ohio Civil Rights Commission. (Def.'s Answer & Counterclaim at P 15). Ms. Masten agreed to withdraw her own charge against the Defendant? if she would not be harassed any further; however, she continued to assist Ms. Thompson. Ms. Masten attempted to drop her charge with the OCRC but on or around July 11, 2011, she learned she was unsuccessful. (Affidavit of Defendant DierdreMasten at P 16-18).

On July 12, 2011, after K&D Management discovered that Ms. Masten was unsuccessful in dropping her OCRC charge, K&D Management retaliated against Ms. Masten by issuing an eighty-one (81) dav notice of non-renewal of lease, requiring her to leave the property on September 30, 2011. (Def.'s Answer & Counterclaim at P 16). Subsequently, K&D Management brought an eviction action in order to coerce, intimidate, threaten, and penalize her for filing complaints with the OCRC when Ms. Masten became a holdover tenant. (Pl.'s Compl.) Ms. Masten filed a timely Answer and Counterclaim alleging retaliatory eviction and discriminatory practices under Fair Housing laws. (Def.'s Answer & Counterclaim).

On February 27, 2012, K&D Management filed a Motion for Summary Judgment arguing for eviction and disposition of Ms. Masten's counterclaims. (Pl.'s Mot. for Summary Judgment).

On May 18, 2012, the OCRC concluded its review of Ms. Masten's claim against K&D Management for the discriminatory conduct she experienced as a result of helping Ms. Thompson, and found probable cause to support Ms. Masten's charges. (Pl.'s Mot. to Supplement the Record).

On August 7, 2012, the court entered the following judgment on K&D Management's motion for summary judgment:

For the following reasons, Plaintiff's motion for summary judgment on count one of its complaint for forcible entry and detainer is granted; however, plaintiff's motion for summary judgment is denied as to count two of Defendant's counterclaim for retaliatory behaviors and retaliatory eviction.

The undisputed facts are that Plaintiff, K&D Management, LLC. ("K&D") is the landlord and managing agent of the premises known as Harbor Crest, an apartment complex located in Euclid, Ohio. Defendant, Deirdre Masten ("Masten") is a tenant of the premises. K&D and Masten entered into a lease agreement for the term of Octoher 5, 2010 through September 30, 2011.

On or about October 15, 2010 Masten assisted another tenant of the premises in filing a charge of discrimination against K&D with the Ohio Civil Rights Commission. Masten subsequently filed her own charge of discrimination against K&D with the Ohio Civil Rights Commission.

On July 12, 2011 K&D served Masten with an eighty-one day notice of nonrenewal of lease, effective September 30, 2011. When Masten did not timely vacate the premises, K&D served her with a three day notice to vacate pursuant to O.R.C. § 1923.04 for the reason that Defendant was a holdover tenant. On October 14, 2011 K& D filed a Complaint for forcible entry and detainer in Euclid Municipal Court. Masten Answered K&D's Complaint and filed a Counterclaim for discriminatory practices, retaliartory behaviors, and retaliatory eviction on November 8, 2011. Euclid Municipal Court then transferred the case to Cuyahoga Country Court of Common Pleas.

It is well settled that a landlord is required to follow a three-step process before a court will order a tenant to vacate the premises. Siegler v. Batdorff, 63 Ohio App.2d 76 (8th dist. 1979). The landlord must provide (1) a notice of termination of tenancy; (2) a notice to vacate the premises; and then the landlord must tile (3) a Compliant in forcible entry and detainer. Indian Hills Senior Community v. Sanders, 8th dist. No. 78780, 2001 Ohio App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001).

In this instance, K&D followed the requisite three step process. Pursuant to the terms of the lease, on July 12, 2011 K&D sent Masten notice of nonrenewal of the lease. Masten failed to vacate the premises on September 30, 2011, where after K&D served Masten with a notice to vacate the premises on October 10, 2011. When Masten failed to vacate the premises, K&D then filed a Complaint for forcible entry and detainer.

In addition to finding that K&D followed the three-step process, the court must also determine whether K&D's act of not renewing the lease constitutes retaliatory conduct. O.R.C. § 5321.02 generally prohibits retaliatory conduct by landlords. Notwithstanding section 5321.02, O.R.C. 5321.03(A)(4) permits a landlord to bring an action for possession of the premises if the tenant is holding over the tenant's term. On September 30, 2011 Masten's lease expired pursuant to the terms of the lease. K&D sent notice to Masten that it did not plan to renew her lease; thus, Masten was a holdover tenant.

The Eighth District Court of Appeals has held that retaliatory conduct of the landlord may not be raised as a defense in a forcible entry detainer proceeding when the tenant is holding over his term. See Siegler and Indian Hills, supra. Accordingly, the Court finds Plaintiff's motion for summary judgment on count one for forcible entry and detainer to be well taken and granted. Masten shall vacate the premises within 30 days of this entry. On evidence presented to the Court at this stage, Plaintiff's request for summary judgment on count two of Defendant's counterclaim is not well taken and is denied. * * * K&D Management, LLC. v. Masten, Cuyahoga C.P. No. CV-11-771214 (Aug. 7, 2012).

And Masten now brings this appeal.

STATEMENT OF ISSUES PRESENTED FOR REVIEW

I. The Trial Court erred in applying Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8th Dist. No. 78780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001), to a holdover tenant who alleges that the Nonrenewal of a Lease was in violation of the Federal Fair Housing Act, 42 U.S.C '3601 et.seq.

II. The Trial Court Decision applying Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8th Dist. No. 78780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001) to the Federal Fair Housing Law violates the Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution.

III. The Trial Court erred in holding that Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8th Dist. No. 78780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001), applies to a holdover tenant who alleges that the Nonrenewal of a Lease was in violation of the Ohio Fair Housing Act, Chapter 4112 of the Ohio Revised Code.

IV. The decision in Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8'h Dist. No. 78780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001), was wrongly decided and should be reversed.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

The Federal Fair Housing Act provides Ohio tenants against discriminatory or retaliatory evictions based on the landlord not renewing a lease for reasons that violation of 42 U.S. C. Sections 3601 et. seq. In addition, federal law under the Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution prevents the use of state case precedent or law to prevent Ohio tenants for receiving the protections afforded them. The Trial Court's granting of summary judgment was improper due to the application of Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8th Dist. No.78780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001) to the Federal Fair Housing Act

Further, the Ohio Fair Housing Act Chapter 4112 does not contain the Ohio Landlord-Tenant Law language found in O.R.C. Section 5321.03 (A) (d) limiting the defense of retaliation in nonrenewal of lease situations. As a result of the misapplication of this section of the Ohio Landlord-Tenant Law the Trial Court applied Indian Hills v. Sanders to the Ohio Fair Housing law, the trial court's order to partially grant K&D Management's motion for summary judgment on this ground should also be set aside.

Finally, based on Ohio Public Policy arguments the holding of Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8th Dist. No. 78780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001) should be reversed even in strictly Ohio Landlord-Tenant Act retaliation claims.

ARGUMENT

I.  The Trial Court Erred in Applying Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8th Dist. No. 78780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001), to a Holdover Tenant who Alleges that the Nonrenewal of a Lease was in Violation of the Federal Fair Housing Act, 42 U.S.C '3601 et.seq.

The landlord's actions in retaliating against Ms. Masten violate the Federal Fair Housing Act, which prohibit retaliation. 42 U.S.C § 3617, of the Fair Housing Act clearly states that:

It shall be unlawful to coerce, intimidate, threaten, or interfere with any person in the exercise or enjoyment of, or on account of his having exercised or enjoyed, or on account of his having aided or encouraged any other person in the exercise or enjoyment of. any right granted or protected by section 3603, 3604, 3605, or 3606 of this title.42 U.S.C. § 3617.

Under § 3617. the term "interfere with" does not require "a showing of "potent force or duress", but was worthy of a broader interpretation that includes the actions of those who are in a position to, and do, directly disrupt the exercise or enjoyment of a protected right and who do so with a discriminatory animus." Byrd v. Brandeburg, 922 F.Supp. 60 (N.D. Ohio 1996). 1 The court in Matarese found that § 3617 extended to situations involving a wide range of circumstances, such as exclusionary zoning practices, deflated appraisals, and insurance redlining. Matarese v. Archstone Pentagon City, 795 F. Supp. 2d 402, 442-43 (E.D. Va. 2011).

The landlord's allegation that they are merely evicting a holdover tenant dramatically misstates Deirdre Masten's position. Ms. Masten observed discrimination of Ms. Thompson, a fellow tenant of the landlord, based on her physical and mental disabilities, and learned of the plan to evict Ms. Thompson for those disabilities. The landlord's conduct towards Ms. Thompson violates the Fair Housing Act, which states, "It shall be unlawful to discriminate in the sale or rental, or to otherwise make unavailable or deny, a dwelling to any buyer or renter because of a handicap of that buyer or renter." 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(1)[emphasis added]. The Plaintiff began retaliating against Ms. Masten's for helping Ms. Thompson exercise her rights under the Fair Housing Act to file a charge with the Ohio Civil Rights Commission, and when Ms. Masten began exercising her own rights by also filing such a charge. Ms. Masten's 81 day nonrenewal of her lease leading to this eviction is the final link in a chain of retaliatory actions, began when Ms. Masten started helping Ms. Thompson

42 U.S.C § 3617 certainly applies to situations where a landlord forces a tenant out of her home as punishment for exercising a right, and/or assisting another in doing the same. Thus, Plaintiff's non-renewal of Ms. Masten's lease on account of her exercising of protected rights is a discriminatory practice and violates 42 U.S.C. § 3617.

Nor, is this an untested theory since it has been recognized in both federal and state courts. For example, in Matarese v. Archstone Pentagon City 795 F. Supp.2d 402, 442-43(E.D. VA 2011) the court held that the defendant property owners had retaliated against the plaintiff in violation of § 3617 of the federal Fair Housing Act, when the defendant refused to renew the plaintiff's lease following her "exercise of protected rights". In determining the standard for retaliation, the court explained that in order to prove that the activity was retaliatory, the plaintiff had to prove that the plaintiff was engaged in protected activity; the defendant was aware of that activity; the defendant took adverse action against the plaintiff; and that a causal connection exists between the protected activity and the adverse action. Id. at 443. The court reasoned that a causal connection was established when the defendant in that case failed to renew the plaintiff's lease following repeated refusal to make accommodations for her chemical sensitivities. The defendant's reasons for the non-renewal were inconsistent, and the court noted its manifestation "only after the exercise of protected activity". Id.

Here, similar to the case in Materese, it is apparent that the reasons given for the Defendant's eviction are pretextual, and that it was a retaliatory action. In its probable cause finding, the Ohio Civil Rights Commission ("OCRC") determined that the Defendant was "subject to harassment, acts or retaliation and referred to as 'crazy'" subsequent to the Plaintiff learning that the Defendant assisted another tenant with a discrimination charge (07/27/2012, DEIRDRE MASTEN MOTION TO SUPPLEMENT THE RECORD AND LAW REGARDING PLTF'S SUMMARY JUDGMENT MOTION O.C.R.C. Determination attached as Exhibit B, pg 1). The Ms. Masten received the notice that her lease would not be renewed on July 12, 2011, the day after she discovered that she would be unable to withdraw her OCRC complaint (Id. at Exhibit B, pg. 3). The landlord's actions occurred following the "exercise of protected activity" and supported the Commission's determination that illegal retaliation had occurred.

II.  The Trial Court Decision Applying Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8th Dist. No. 78 780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001) to the Federal Fair Housing Law Violates the Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution.

In Michigan Canners & Freezers Assn., Inc. v. Agricultural Marketing and Bargaining Board, 467 U.S. 461, 469, 104 S.Ct. 2518, 2522-23, 81 L.Ed.2d 399 (1984), the U.S. Supreme Court held that federal law preempts state law through explicit preemption, when it indicates the intent to occupy an entire field of regulation which preempt state regulation in that area, and when state law actually conflicts with federal laws. Under 42 U.S.C. § 3615 of the Federal Fair Housing Act any state law "that purports to require or permit any action that would be a discriminatory housing practice under this subchapter shall to that extent be invalid." 42 U.S.C. § 3615.

Thus even if this Court would find that Indian Hills v. Sander, supra it could not be used to deny Ms. Masten's right to raise this federally protected right to raise the defense of retaliatory eviction since this section of the FHA protects Ms. Masten's conduct. The landlord's retaliatory non-renewal of Ms. Masten's lease and the subsequent eviction violates 42 U.S.C. § 3617 giving rise to the defense.

To the extent that O.R.C. 5321.03 as applied by the Trial Court is alleged to apply to this part of the Federal Fair Housing Act it is invalid to if it would permit a discriminatory housing practice. Any interpretation of R.C. 5321.03 that would permit the landlord to retaliate against Ms. Masten, is explicitly pre-empted, and rendered invalid. Consequently, Ms. Masten's defense and counterclaim for retaliation is preserved.

Ohio Courts under the Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution are required to adhere to federal law when state case precedent is inconsistent with federal decisions. "The Supremacy Clause of the Constitution requires that if a state measure conflicts with a federal requirement, the state provision must give way." See Swift & Co. v. Wickham, 382 U.S. Ill, 120 (1965).

In the present case, Ms. Masten has the protection under the Supremacy Clause to raise this federally recognized defense to an eviction for discriminatory nonrenewal under the Fair Housing Act. See 42 U.S.C § § 3604 and 3617. K&D Management's attempt to use of the Ohio Landlord-Tenant Act ("state law") is inconsistent with the Federal Fair Housing Act. Therefore, Ohio Courts must apply federal law to Ms. Masten defense of discriminatory practices and retaliatory eviction.

This principle has been applied to the Federal Fair Housing Act. In United States v. Wisconsin, 395 F. Supp. 732, 732 (1975), the U.S. Department of Justice brought suit after passage of Wisconsin Statute § 101.22(4m). The state law made it a crime to underting fair housing testing in Wisconsin. This state law conflicted with the Federal Fair Housing Act of 1968. The court found that the statute was in conflict with the "general scheme" of the Fair Housing Act of 1968 and thus was unconstitutional under the Supremacy Clause of Article VI of the United States Constitution. The state was enjoined from enforcing the state statute and was required to give a copy of the court's order to various state and federal agencies affected by the court's ruling. As in Wisconsin, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit Court has ruled that the Fair Housing Act expressly prevents the operation of state laws that it conflicts with. See Larkin v. State of Michigan Dept. of Social Serv., 89 F. 3d 285, 289 (6th Cir. 1996).

In Larkin, the plaintiff applied for a license for an adult foster care (AFC) facility under the Michigan Adult Foster Care Licensing Act and the defendant denied her application because the proposed AFC facility was within 1,500 feet of another AFC facility and therefore was not in compliance with the spacing requirements of Michigan state law. The plaintiff brought suit against the defendant on the above grounds that the spacing requirements provided for under Michigan law was in conflict with the Fair Housing Act and Fair Housing Amendments Act. The court affirmed the judgment granting the motion of plaintiff for summary judgment against the state social services department. The court held that Fair Housing Act and the Fair Housing Amendments Act preempted the discriminatory spacing and notice requirements of the Michigan Adult Foster Care Facility Licensing Act, which were not tailored to the needs of disabled adults.

Like in Larkin, the Ohio law in which defendant in the Masten case builds its case must give way to the Fair Housing Act which does not permit retaliation or discrimination. 3 Thus, this Court should reverse the Trial Court ruling finding that the federally protected right to raise retaliatory eviction defense in a fair housing claim must be recognized in the instant case. Ohio Courts are required to follow the Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution and apply the Fair Housing Act of 1968 when deciding cases where Ohio law is inconsistent with the federal law.

III.  The Trial Court Erred in Holding that Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8th Dist No. 78780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001), Applies to a Holdover Tenant who Alleges that the Nonrenewal of a Lease was in Violation of the Ohio Fair Housing Act, Chapter 4112 of the Ohio Revised Code.

The two cases cited by the landlord in their Motion for Summary Judgment Indian Hills Senior Community, Inc. v. Sanders 8th Dist. Ct. of App. No 78780, unreported 2001 WL 958055(Aug 23, 2001) and Associated Estates Realty Corp. v. Samsa 8thDist. Ct. of App. 2004-ohio-6635(Dec 9, 2004) should not apply to the defense of retaliatory eviction for nonrenewal of a lease under the Ohio Fair Housing Act. O.R.C. Chapter 4112.02 et seq.

In Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8th Dist. No. 78780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001) the tenant entered into a lease with Indian Hills on Feb 17, 1998 (two year lease, March 1, 1998 - Feb 28, 2000). On January 3, 2000, Sanders was delivered a letter saying her lease was not being renewed and subsequently the tenant was given an eviction notice, demanding she leave by March 7, 2000. When she did not leave the landlord, Indian Hills, filed an eviction with Euclid court; Sanders answered and filed a counterclaim that the nonrenewal was retaliatory since she was a part of a tenant organization that complained about violations of building and safety codes. In May 2000, the court granted Indian Hills' Summary Judgment.

Sanders argued on appeal that Indian Hills' retaliatory action was the non-renewal. A panel of this Court held that Indian Hills met the three standards set out in Seigler v. Batdorff (1979) 63 Ohio App.2d 76,408 N.E.2d 1383. 4 The tenant had contended that on March 1, 2000 she became a month-to-month tenant. This Court reasoned that she would have becoming a month-to-month if not for the notice of non-renewal given in January.

On the issue of whether the non-renewal was retaliatory, the court lists the situations in which a retaliatory eviction is illegal. However, § 5321.02 ("a landlord may not retaliate against a tenant by increasing the tenant's rent, decreasing services that are due to the tenant, or bringing or threatening to bring an action for possession of the tenant's premises ") precludes nonrenewal. [See 5321.03 (A) (d)]

A similar holding was made in a subsequent decision of this Court. In Associated Estates Realty Corp. v. Samsa 8th Dist. Ct. of App. 2004-ohio-6635(Dec 9, 2004) Jacob Samsa was on a year lease (beginning April 1983) that converted to a month-to-month lease in May 1991. On September 4, 2002, Samsa was given a 45-day notice of eviction. The lease "expired" on November 1, 2002 and Samsa would not leave. The landlord filed an eviction claim and Samsa counterclaimed that the Associated Estates didn't repair and maintain the residence in a safe manner and claimed retaliatory eviction for complaining about such things.

The Trial court granted Summary Judgment, saying Samsa failed to connect his eviction with the complaints, and not to the holdover status. Samsa unsuccessfully appealed claiming notice provisions were not met. Claiming the lease provided for notice to be given by mail (it was delivered by hand in this case).

This Court brought up its opinion from Indian Hills decided three years earlier. "Just like in Indian Hills, Samsa became a holdover tenant when he failed to move out at the end of the lease after Appellee exercised its rights under the lease to not renew the lease."

Our facts are significantly different from these two cases. Unlike the Ohio Landlord-Tenant Act the Ohio Fair Housing Act. O.R.C. Chapter 4112.02 et seq. does not contain the language found in Section 5321.03 that "[n]otwithstanding section 5321.02 of the Revised Code [dealing with defense of retaliatory eviction], a landlord may bring an action under Chapter 1923. of the Revised Code for possession of the premises if: (4) A tenant is holding over the tenant's term." Therefore, whatever the validity of Indian Hills Senior Community, Inc. v. Sanders and Associated Estates Realty Corp. v. Samsa without this particularized language its rationale should not apply to the Ohio Fair Housing Act defense of retaliatory eviction for nonrenewal.

Finally, the interpretation of the Ohio Fair Housing Act by Ohio courts often is based on federal precedent. Wooten v. Columbus, Div. of Water, 91 Ohio App. 3d 326 (10'" Dist.1993) (discussing the appropriateness of looking to analogous federal law for guidance). This does not mean that the Ohio law is identical. In Matthews v. New Century Mortg. Corp.,185 F.Supp.2d 874 (S.D. Ohio 2002) the court points out "Toledo Fair Hous. Or. v. Nationwide Mut. Ins. Co., 94 Ohio Misc.2d 14, (1993) (recognizing that, while the language of the Ohio statute closely parallels the language of the FHA, 'the Ohio Civil Rights Act appears to contain language more favorable for maintaining a housing discrimination suit than the federal Fair Housing Act". Based both on the lack of the language limiting the use of retaliatory defense in holdovers found in the Ohio Landlord-Tenant Act and the federal precedent permitting such a defense under the Federal Fair Housing Act this Court should reverse the Trial Court's granting of summary judgment. It will be illogical to apply a section of state law to another statute that does not have any such limiting language.

IV.  The Decision in Indian Hills v. Sanders, 8th Dist No. 78780, 2001 App. Lexis 3717 (August 23, 2001), was Wrongly Decided and Should be Reversed.

In many instances, a non-renewal of a lease equates to an eviction. The non-renewal constitutes, like an eviction, adverse conduct on the part of the landlord and will have common effects on the tenant. The tenant is often left without a place to live and it may be necessary for the tenant to remain on the premises so as to avoid becoming indigent. The reason for the nonrenewal of a lease or eviction may constitute an illegal action, such as retaliation. Many tenants who are subject to evictions or lease non-renewals do not possess the knowledge concerning their legal rights and potential causes of action, or the knowledge related to finding the resources for finding this information. It may take time for such a tenant to become aware there is legal recourse for the treatment to which they may have been unfairly subjected. This information may come to the tenant when they are categorized as a holdover tenant. It is grossly inequitable to deny such tenants legal recourse for the potential wrong that has been done them. Additionally, it is not inequitable to allow holdover tenants to have standing on the given causes of actions as a prima facie sound complaint is required to survive summary judgment.

The Supreme Court of Ohio has stated that "public policy is that principle of law which holds that no person can lawfully do that which has a tendency to be injurious to the public or against the public good * * *." Porter v. Trustees of Cincinnati Southern Railway, 96 Ohio St. 29, 33-34, 117 N.E. 20, 21. In Porter the Court reasoned a public policy violation is measured by the "tendency of the contract to injure the public good rather than by actual injury under the particular circumstances."

The landlord's purpose in non-renewing Ms. Masten's lease and subsequently filing for eviction was '"injurious to the public or contravenes some established interest of society." L'Oranse v. Medical Protective Co. (CCA. 6. 19681), 394 F. 2d 57, 60. quoting McCullough Transfer Co. v. Virginia Surety Co., Inc., 213 F.2d.440, 443 (6th Cir. 199).

The Plaintiff in L 'Orange, a doctor, testified for the plaintiff in a malpractice action. The Defendant terminated his insurance in retaliation. The Sixth circuit reasoned the Defendant's actions violated public policy because:

A member of the medical profession could hardly be expected to appear in court and testify for a plaintiff in any litigation if the penalty might be the cancellation of his own malpractice insurance. It manifestly is contrary to public policy to permit an insurance company to use policy cancellation as punishment against a doctor or dentist who appears as a witness to protect the rights of a plaintiff who has been wronged by another member of the profession. If the insurance industry can use the cancellation procedure to keep members of the medical profession from testifying as witnesses, malpractice litigation can be stifled.

L 'Orange ruled that the cancellation of his insurance violates Ohio public policy "insofar as it tends to threaten him as a witness in pending and future lawsuits." The court then ruled that the purpose for which the power of cancellation was used rendered the cancellation void, and constituted a breach of contract, because otherwise the insurer would be able to "to invoke the cancellation clause for the purpose of intimidating and threatening a witness, an act which is prohibited by the public policy of Ohio."

Ms. Masten's factual situation is identical to that of the Plaintiff in L 'Orange. Ms. Masten was engaged in helping Ann Thompson exercise her rights under both Federal and State Fair Housing laws. She, a tenant, worked to protect the rights of another tenant, injured by her landlord. Ms. Masten helped draft Ms. Thompson's' complaint and planned to appear as a witness. The Plaintiff retaliated by choosing not renewal her lease, and subsequently beginning eviction proceedings when the lease expired.

The public policy that protects Ms. Masten from eviction is declared in the "clear consciousness and conviction of what is naturally and inherently just and right between man and man." Pittsburgh, Cincinnati, Chicago & St. Louis Railway Co. v. Kinney, 95 Ohio St. 64, 68, 115 N.E. 505, 507, L.R.A. 1917D, 641. Allowing the landlord to invoke their power of nonrenewal of the lease, and subsequently evict Ms. Masten's due to her decision to help a fellow tenant, is tantamount to allowing the Plaintiff to use it as a tool of witness intimidation. This could lead to tenants fearing to speak up and assist other tenants who have been victimized by their landlords. This is not inherently just and if left unchecked could amount to a grievous "injury to the public." L 'Orange v. Medical Protective Co. 394 F.2d 57, 60. Therefore, the use of the cancellation policy in the lease, and the subsequent eviction proceedings, even if proper, should be considered void as a violation of public policy.

The holding of Indian Hills to provide a safe harbor for landlords retaliating against tenants for actions protected under 5321.02 of the Ohio Revised Code creates an injustice. The retaliatory action is the non-renewal of a lease or the subsequent eviction. The Indian Hill ruling effectively negates, after a very short period of time, any chance the tenant subject to the retaliation has to file the cause of action provided by 5321.02. Indian Hills has a chilling effect on tenants exercising their rights under 5321.02 of the Revised Code. The essence of retaliation is an act by a party in response to a perceived adverse act of another party. If a tenant knows the price for engaging in the activities protected in 5321.02 may be a non-renewal of their lease or eviction, and an avenue for legal recourse is excluded, the tenant will elect not to participate in the activities protected by 5321.02. This is undesirable not only for the negative effects it may have on the tenant in question, but it effectively limits a reporting mechanism used by housing code enforcement authorities to detect violations of the law.

Ms. Masten urges that the holding in Indian Hills is over inclusive and sets a dangerous precedent by not allowing harmed tenants to have the legal right to protect their an interest in having a decent and safe apartment or home. It is urged that the case be reversed as against public policy.

Based on the legal arguments as well as the reasoning stated above the Appellant Deidre Masten urges the reversal of the granting of summary judgment by the Trial Court of Plaintiff's Count One of its Complaint. This matter should be remanded for discovery and trial on the merits of her defenses and counterclaims.
